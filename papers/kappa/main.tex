\begin{abstract}
Spatial information is crucial to the listener's experience of music, and stereophonic recordings have long been the standard for capturing this spatiality. Recent advancements in computing, coupled with the availability of new, very large musical corpora, have created opportunities for large-scale quantitative analysis of the stereo image of recorded music. This licentiate thesis addresses a key gap in current research: direct analysis of raw music recordings to describe the stereo image. It will explore existing methods, assess their effectiveness, and demonstrate potential use cases for spatial analysis within audio reproduction, transmission and musical performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

It is a widely accepted truth in the world of music production and recording that spatial information in audio is crucial to  listeners' sense of immersion and connection with the music. To capture that spatiality, stereophonic recording and reproduction has been the standard format since it gained popularity in the 1950s \cite{lanierHIFITWOCHANNELCOMMOTION1957}. The spatial analysis of subsequent decades worth of stereo recordings presents a unique opportunity to study human musicality, as each recording reflects and encodes the creative choices made by the musicians and recordists involved.

Within the music community, it is common to speak about spatiality in a qualitative sense, using terms such as \enquote{stereo image} and \enquote{stereo field} to encompass all audible spatial aspects. There have been several attempts to translate these discussions in a quantitive domain \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007,dewhirstModellingPerceivedSpatial2008,sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008,tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011}, all of which were performed in an era when musical datasets were hand-curated/-labelled, fairly small (1,000 - 2,000 tracks), and -- most notably -- private. However, in recent years new musical corpora have become available, e.g. MTG-Jamendo \cite{bogdanovMTGJamendoDatasetAutomatic2019} and \gls{fma} \cite{defferrardFMADatasetMusic2017}, which are both orders of magnitude larger than their predecessors, containing approximately 55,000 and 100,000 tracks respectively. In addition, computing techniques in \gls{ml} and \gls{dl} have progressed to the point where it is now possible to learn extremely high-level features on these vast datasets, thereby making sense of the human creativity represented in the recordings. 

A typical use case for spatial descriptors that has emerged both in academia and industry is classification, and in particular, auto-tagging of musical genre/performer \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningInformation2010,ziemerGoniometersArePowerful2023}. Such features can be used in music similarity and recommendation tasks \cite{gomezMusicSimilaritySystems2008,ziemerNovelRecordingStudio2020}. It has also been shown that stereo field classification of subsections within a larger work can act as a precursor to other tasks such as segmentation, audio enhancement, upmixing, and source separation \cite{barryMusicStructureSegmentation2007,harmaStereoAudioClassification2011,harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2011}. The latter is very relevant to the audio distribution industry, which is currently experimenting with newer audio formats such as ambisonic and object-based audio. While these new formats offer exciting opportunities for both distributors and listeners, there remains a huge back-catalogue of stereophonic recordings that could benefit from a high-level understanding of their spatial content. 

Historically, binaural audio (which is a subclass of stereo) has been of particular interest because its relevance to human hearing. Thus, it is extremely widely studied, and the underlying fundamental techniques are well-established \cite{blauertTechnologyBinauralListening2013}. Yet there remains a gap in current research regarding direct analysis of raw music recordings, and in particular, describing the stereo image of said recordings at a high level. This thesis aims to fill that gap. It will firstly demonstrate use cases where the spatial analysis of stereo audio has the opportunity to solve real-world problems in audio transmission, dissemination, and reproduction. Accordingly, it will gather existing methods to describe stereo image and assess their abilities to detect the spatial aspects of a variety of musical audio sources.

\section{Research Questions and Contributions}
\label{sec:request_questions}

The underlying purpose of the research documented in this thesis relates to \gls{mir}, and in particular to generating an understanding of the links between human musical creativity and musical output. While it might be possible for the creative people involved to explain how they make their music, it is far more objective to analyse their output as a representation of their musical ideas.
\\\\
\noindent Given this context, the overarching research question can be formed as:

\begin{quote}
How do music producers use stereo field?
\end{quote}

\noindent ...which can be broken into several smaller problems:

\begin{enumerate}
\item How does the stereo image of a recording relate to real-world musical knowledge? \label{rq:usecase}
\item What methods can be used to describe the stereo image of music recording? \label{rq:methods}
\end{enumerate}

One could argue that the overarching question might be answered by interviewing music producers, but this would quickly run into a problem of scale. The two smaller research questions were therefore designed to allow the overarching question to be answered by objective analysis on musical recordings - something that is abundant nowadays.

\section{Background}

\subsection{The mechanics of human hearing}
\label{sec:hearing}

\citeauthor{lindsayHumanInformationProcessing1972}'s book \citetitle{lindsayHumanInformationProcessing1972} \cite{lindsayHumanInformationProcessing1972} gives a detailed description of how the human ear is constructed and how it enables us to hear. The relevant parts are summarized below.

The ear contains three major sections, loosely labelled the \q{outer}, \q{middle}, and \q{inner} ear. When air vibrations (i.e. sound waves) flow into the ear, mechanical coupling in the middle ear transmits vibrations to the cochlea (which the major organ of the inner ear). The cochlea is a conical tube which is coiled up into a spiral, filled with an incompressible fluid, and covered on either end by a membrane. Through the centre of the tube runs the basilar membrane, which is a covered in hairs named cilia. When vibrations are mechanically transmitted to the cochlea, the internal fluid responds by transmitting those vibrations to the basilar membrane, and the cilia will bend and twist accordingly. The cilia are linked to nerve fibres, and will initiate electrical pulses when manipulated. These electrical pulses are sent to the brain and interpreted as sounds.

Along the length of the cochlea, the cilia have different \q{critical frequencies} that they respond to. The critical frequency increases logarithmically as one moves from the base to the apex of the cochlea. This is what gives humans their characteristic 20-20000Hz hearing range.

\subsection{Directional hearing}
Following from \cref{sec:hearing}, this section will discuss humans' ability to discern the direction or location of a sound source.

The psychoacoustic theory behind spatial hearing has been an active area of research since the mid 20th century, with early examples including the Jeffress model in which time differences between sound events at each ear contribute to a person's ability to locate sounds in space \cite{jeffressPlaceTheorySound1948}. The work of \citeauthor{lindemannEvaluationInterauralSignal1982} a few decades later expanded on this idea by translating the earlier theory to a \gls{dsp} context, and establishing the \gls{ild}, \gls{itd}, and \gls{iacc} as important measures to describe the spatial aspects of binaural signals in a quantifiable way \cite{lindemannEvaluationInterauralSignal1982}.

Put simply, humans are able to perceive the incoming direction of a sound by observing both the amplitude difference and time difference of coincident sound waves arriving at the left and right ears. Figure \cref{fig:directional_hearing} shows how these two properties enable directional hearing. Firstly, the head itself creates a physical barrier that will attenuate sound waves at one ear more-so that at the other ear. Secondly, the difference in distance between the sound source and each ear will create a delay in receiving the sound at either ear. Over time a person will learn to associate patterns in \gls{itd}/\gls{ild} with the physical location of the sound source.

The relative importance of \gls{ild} and \gls{itd} differ depending on the frequency of the incoming sound. The reasons for this are twofold. Firstly, a person's inter-aural distance (the physical distance between their ears) must be longer than the wavelength of the incoming sound wave in order for them to detect the phase difference between wave peaks. Secondly the hair cells inside the human cochlea can only detect the fine structure of a sound wave up until roughly 1.6kHz \cite{lindemannEvaluationInterauralSignal1982}. \citeauthor{lindemannEvaluationInterauralSignal1982} gives a rule of thumb that time differences of less than 7.5ms are heard as a single auditory event (for which direction could be inferred), and delays above 21ms are heard as uncorrelated events. Hence, at 1.6kHz and below \gls{itd} is more important, whereas above 1.6kHz \gls{ild} is more important. Furthermore, \citeauthor{blauertSpatialHearingOne1996}'s subsequent work found firstly that both \gls{ild} and \gls{itd} are relevant within a frequency band, and secondly that the angle of incidence of the sound wave plays a role in how the cues are perceived \cite{blauertSpatialHearingOne1996}. Despite this, the aforementioned rules of thumb are still broadly accepted, even being encoded into international standards such as \cite{iso-11172-3:mpeg_audio}. As an example, \cref{fig:example_pan} shows two recordings (a low-frequency kick drum and high-frequency hi-hat) that originate from the right-hand side of a listener.

\subsection{Loudness perception}
Humans perceive the loudness of sound, not in terms of the absolute amplitude of the sound wave, but the \textit{power} of the sound wave. Mathematically speaking, perceived loudness is proportional to the \gls{rms} \gls{spl} raised to an exponent \cite{mooreIntroductionPsychologyHearing2013}. Observing that the calculation of \gls{rms} naturally involves taking the square of the original time function (see \cref{eq:rms}), it follows multiplication of a signal by $\sqrt{2}$ will result in double perceived loudness. Conversely, multiplying a signal by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ will halve the perceived loudness.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:rms}
    f_{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T_2 - T_1} \int_{T_1}^{T_2}[f(t)^2]dt}
\end{equation}

In recording studio vernacular, this is call \q{panning}. In accordance with the two signal differences a that humans are able to detect, there are also two basic types of panning -- amplitude panning and time panning. By way of example, \cref{fig:phantom_amplitude} shows how the phantom position can be moved anywhere in between the speakers that are reproducing the sound by applying different amplitude gains to the left and right channel of a stereo recording.

Another frequently used technique is \q{binaural panning}, which assigns combinations of amplitude and time difference to each stereo channel. This is typically achieved by applying a \gls{hrir} to the raw audio signal. An \gls{hrir} is pair of impulse responses recorded with microphones placed in the ears of either a real person or a dummy head. To obtain an \gls{hrir}, a known test signal is played from a specific position in a room, and is recorded using the previously mentioned binaural microphones. The \gls{hrir} can then be convolved with any other monophonic recording to make it appear as though it came from the position of the original sound source.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/binaural_hrir.pdf}
    \caption{Illustration of a binaural \gls{hrir} being recorded.}
    \label{fig:phantom_binaural}
\end{figure}


In traditional recording studio setups, the recording engineer or producer will use one or more microphones to capture an original sound source. These singular sound sources are commonly called \q{stems}. During the production process, the stems may be panned to create a sense of space in the final mix. In the case that the stem was recorded using two microphones, the stem would be called a \q{stereo stem}, and the term \q{panning} is usually replaced by \q{balance}, and only amplitude panning (as opposed to time or binaural panning) would be applied.

Amplitude panning is the most common panning technique is traditional music production \cite{shelvockHearingYourAudience2020}. It is associated with so-called \q{panning laws}, which govern how the amplitude is changed in each channel according the desired pan position \cite{izhakiPanning2023}. One example is the \q{energy-preserving pan law} (sometimes called the \mbox{\q{-3dB pan law}}) in which the squared sum of the channel gains always equals 1. In mathematical terms, this is defined by \cref{eq:equal_power_panning}, where $g_{L,R}$ represent the gains applied to left and right channels, and $a$ is the desired panning position in the range 0 (fully left) to 1 (fully right).

\begin{align}
\label{eq:equal_power_panning}
    g_L &= \cos(\frac{\pi}{2} a) \\
    g_R &= \sin(\frac{\pi}{2} a)
\end{align}

\subsection{Stereo image}
The term \q{stereo image} is used to describe the overall distribution of sound across the horizontal plane after panning and any other production techniques have been applied. One may also use terms like \q{stereo field}, \q{soundstage}, or \q{panorama}, which all refer to the same thing.  They are usually used in qualitative descriptions such as \q{big}, \q{wide}, and \q{ambient} rather than quantitative measures \cite{owsinskiMixingEngineersHandbook2006}. When mixing a musical recording, a producer will pan individual stems to their desired position within the stereo image. Those stems which are recorded in stereo in fact have their own stereo image, which can also be controlled by the producer. The combination of all stems into a final stereo mix, constitutes the overall stereo image. 

\Cref{fig:stereo_image} shows a simple example stereo image, as perceived by the producer. It contains two mono stems -- vocals and violin, and one stereo stem -- piano. There are some common tropes seen throughout many productions, some of which are shown in the diagram. Firstly, vocals are recorded in mono and panned to the centre. Secondly, since the piano is panned slightly left, the violin is panned roughly the same amount to the right in order to achieve a balanced mix. Thirdly, all stems are placed in the stereo image in a way that emulates a natural performance on a stage, and to avoid separate instruments masking each other. Note that while these practices are typical, they are purely an artistic choice on behalf of the producer \cite{owsinskiMixingEngineersHandbook2006}; Recordings breaking the trend are not difficult to find.

Describing the stereo image of a recording has its roots in the acoustics domain, in which typical use cases are the analysis of concert halls and music reproduction systems. In these cases, the previously mentioned low-level spatial cues typically form the basis of the analysis. \Citeauthor{bergSystematicEvaluationPerceived2003} \cite{bergSystematicEvaluationPerceived2003} give a long list of typical perceptual measures that can be derived from the low-level cues, although later literature has focused on the measures of \gls{asw} and \gls{lev}. However, as stated by \citeauthor{sarroffComputationalModelPerceived2011} \cite{sarroffComputationalModelPerceived2011}, the task of describing the spatial aspects of recorded music is inherently different to that of acoustical signals since said recordings may exhibit spatial aspects that cannot occur in physical acoustic space.

\subsection{Music production}
A typical setup for a music producer is to use a \gls{daw} - graphical software that allows them to record and edit audio. There are many different \glspl{daw} available, but one such example (Reaper) is shown in \cref{fig:daw}. In particular, it shows a single recorded audio track which has it's panning automated. It also contains a single effects track which applies a stereo reverb effect to the recorded audio. This effects track has its \q{dry/wet} mix parameter automated, which controls the ratio of non-processed to processed sound. In a more complete recording session, the \gls{daw} would contain many more audio and effects tracks, each with their own panning control.

The assessment of how the two stereo channels interact is of prime importance when producing music, and quality issues that occur when the stereophonic recordings are mixed down into a monophonic signal for reproduction have been documented well before the advent of digital recording \cite{harveyCompatibilityProblemsTwochannel1962}. In more modern production, \gls{daw} plugins are generally used to visualize and assess the stereo qualities of the final mix \cite{bitzerSpatialDistributionMeter2007,ziemerGoniometersArePowerful2023}. \Cref{fig:goniometer} shows one example - the ToneBoosters Goniometer plugin. It is designed to replicate an analogue goniometer, which, taking two voltage signals as input, displays them on a 2D axis such that a music producer might be able to discern the correlation between the signals.

\subsection{Digital audio signals}
When a sound wave is converted into an electrical signal by a microphone, it can be considered to be operating in continuous time. To convert the analogue signal into a digital one, it is sampled at regular intervals. The sampling frequency ($f_s$) is related to the sampling interval ($\Delta$) by the relationship $\Delta = \frac{1}{f_s}$.

Shannon's sampling theorem states that a signal can be perfectly recreated when sampled at twice the highest frequency contained in the signal. Conversely, the highest frequency the highest frequency that can be represented by sampling a signal at a given sampling frequency is half of that frequency. This limit is called the \q{Nyquist frequency} and is calculated by $f_N = \frac{f_s}{2}$ \cite{yaoNyquistFrequency1983}. 

This is illustrated by \Cref{fig:nyquist}, which shows the sampling of three waveforms. The lower plot shows a signal at the Nyquist frequency ($f_N$), which can be perfectly reconstructed from the digital samples. The upper two waveforms have frequencies equidistant but in opposite directions from the Nyquist frequency ($f_N \pm f_{\delta}$). The digital samples shown in circles could represent either of the two waveforms. In practice, when the analogue waveform is reconstructed, the lower frequency will always take precedence.

% When a signal is sampled, the value is defined only at the time of the sample and is undefined for the period in between samples. Therefore, the digital signal in \cref{fig:nyquist} marked by circles could represent either of the two underlying analogue signals $f(t) = \cos((f_N \pm \Delta) t)$.

Now consider the case where $\omega = f_N$. $f[n] = \cos(f_N T_s n) = \cos(\frac{f_s}{2} \frac{1}{fs} n) = \cos(\frac{n}{2})$.

\subsection{Time-Frequency representation of audio}

In order to perform many types of audio analysis, the audio signal can be converted from the time domain into the \gls{tf} domain. In perceptual acoustics research there are several common forms of \gls{tf} representation, each with its own nuances. This section provides those details.

\subsubsection{\gls{stft} spectrogram}

The \gls{stft} spectrogram is one of the most popular \gls{tf} representions across all audio processing work due to its computational efficiency. The fundamental principle is that a time-domain signal is split into short (1 to 100ms) overlapping chunks, and the \gls{fft} is performed on each chunk, thereby converting that chunk into the frequency domain.  By arranging subsequent frames along a time axis it is possible to represent the original signal as a 2D image known as a spectrogram. When the time-domain signal is entirely real, (which is the case for monophonic audio signals), the result of the \gls{fft} will be a complex signal which is symmetrical around the Nyquist frequency and the upper half is therefore dropped. Furthermore, the complex numbers are usually converted into magnitude/phase representation to better match the human auditory system.

The important parameters of the \gls{stft} are
\begin{itemize}
    \item Frame size - The number of samples in each audio chunk. This must be a power of 2 to gain maximal computational efficiency.
    \item Hop size - The number of sample between the start of subsequent frames. Sometimes expressed as a percentage of the frame size.
\end{itemize}

The frame size dictates the resolution of the spectrogram's frequency axis since the frequency bins will be spaced evenly between 0Hz and the Nyquist frequency. Importantly, a longer frame size will result in lower time accuracy within each frame; sharp transients in the time domain will appear as wideband energy in the frequency domain, but information about exactly where in the frame the transient occurred is lost. To counteract this inaccuracy, a smaller hop size can be used so that transients might be detected as sharp changes between \gls{stft} frames. \Cref{fig:stft_accuracy} shows this trade-off for a short vocal sample. Note that as the frame size increases there is a loss in time accuracy, with smaller hop sizes partially able to counteract. The appropriate values of frame and hop size should therefore be chosen based on knowledge of the signal being analysed.

A fundamental property of the \gls{fft} is that it assumes that the signal repeats outside the time frame that is being analysed. Since this is generally not the case for audio signals, it is standard practice to apply a time-domain windowing function to each frame so that large transients do not occur at the boundaries. Like the frame and hop sizes, the choice of windowing function should be based on the signal itself and the intended outcome of the analysis. For most audio applications, the \q{Hann} window can be used \cite{LibrosastftLibrosa0110}.

\subsubsection{Mel spectrogram}

One major drawback of the \gls{stft} spectrogram is that the frequency bins are spaced linearly from 0Hz to the Nyquist frequency, whereas the human perception of pitch works on a logarithmic scale. The \gls{stft} therefore contains redundant information in higher frequencies. One solution is to divide the entire spectrum into frequency bands that are spaced logarithmically within the entire spectrum and calculate weighted averages of the frequency bins within each band. The standard method is to calculate a \q{Mel filter bank}, an example of which is shown in \cref{fig:mel_filters}. When the Mel filter bank is applied to an \gls{stft} spectrogram, the result is called a \q{Mel spectrogram}. An example is shown in \cref{fig:spect_comp}. Note that information in the higher frequencies has been condensed more-so that the lower frequencies.

\subsubsection{Cochleagram}

As an alternative to the two spectrogram representations, a cochleagram aims to make a \gls{tf} representation that models the acoustic response of the human cochlea more closely. To that end, all processing is performed in the time domain and is sometimes referred to as a \q{peripheral ear model}. Several examples appear in the literature \cite{boddenBinauralModelingAuditory1995,choiObjectiveMeasurementPerceived2008,delgadoDesignChoicesBinaural2023}. In the classical method \cite{boddenBinauralModelingAuditory1995} the original signal is first processed by a bank of time-domain gammatone filters with their centre frequencies placed according to a perceptual scale and each sub-band is subsequently processed by a half-wave rectifier, square root, and low-pass filter to mimic the excitation patterns of the cilia and basilar membrane. Each time-domain signal is then divided into overlapping time segments. The amplitude of each \gls{tf} segment can then be calculated as the mean of all values its values, forming one pixel in the cochleagram. \Cref{fig:spect_comp} shows a comparison of all spectrogram types.

\subsection{Machine learning}
The large scale of the vast music repositories being addressed by the research questions naturally implies that some sort of machine learning is necessary. Broadly speaking, machine learning techniques can be broken into two classes - supervised and unsupervised. The former requires the training dataset to be labelled, while the latter does not. In relation to research question 1, we assume that a music producer's \gls{daw} session is the only source of objective ground-truth data. Furthermore, we assume that \gls{daw} sessions are not publicly available, as was found in \cite{colonelReverseEngineeringRecording2021,rodriguezSpectralDynamicConstellations2024}; The only public-facing output of a recording session is the mixdown. It follows then that any spatial information derived from a mixdown will be unlabelled data and will therefore require unsupervised learning in order to make sense of it.


\section{Related works}

\subsection{Stereo image description}

\fxerror{Binaural analysis, \cite{supperOnsetGuidedSpatialAnalyser2005,takanenBinauralAuditoryModel2012,antoniukBlindEstimationEnsemble2023}}

Early examples of the use of spatial cues for analysis of recorded music are the \enquote{\gls{pi}} and \enquote{ambience index} specified by \citeauthor{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002} \cite{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002}, which are loosely analogous to \gls{ild} and \gls{iacc} in the acoustics domain. To estimate the \gls{pi}, a signal model is assumed whereby a musical source is panned within the mix using the \enquote{energy preserving pan law}, in which amplitude differences are applied to each channel such that the sum of the squared amplitudes equals 1. While this panning technique does not affect the phase of either channel, existing phase differences between channels are measured using the \enquote{ambience index}, which is defined such that it is low when the stereo channels are correlated (indicating a direct sound source), and high when channels are uncorrelated (indicating an ambient or reverberant sound source). 

As the panning and ambience indices are key to many subsequent works, \cref{fig:pi_ai} demonstrates them on an example music recording. The recording itself is a solo singer recorded in an anechoic chamber, amplitude panned from left to right over the course of the musical phrase. At the midway point, an artificial reverb effect (i.e. a source of ambience) is added and slowly faded in. One can observe firstly that the ambience is low while there is no reverb, then increases steadily in frequency bands where audio energy is present, until finally all frequency bands have high ambience. Secondly, the panning index starts hard left and moves steadily right, becoming more and more unclear as the reverb is faded in.

Importantly, both the panning and ambience index are calculated in the \gls{tf} domain and result in one value for each \gls{tf} bin, and hence enable use cases that require resynthesis or editing of audio after analysis. One such example is the oft-cited \gls{adress} algorithm \cite{barrySoundSourceSeparation2004} (and its subsequent downstream projects \cite{cooneyEnhancedImplementationADRess2006,sofianosEffectiveSingingVoice2010,defreinReformulatingBinaryMasking2020,defreinRemedyingSoundSource2020}) (which attempt to separate sound sources by locating them in the horizontal plane). For the purpose of description, it is arguable that a summary of such spatial cues is more useful.

Using the same signal model proposed by \citeauthor{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002}, the spatial description of recorded music specifically has been attempted several times. Firstly by \citeauthor{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007} in \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007,tzanetakisStereoPanningInformation2010}, who use the \gls{pi} to define a set of \gls{spf} used for genre classification and \gls{mir}. \gls{spf} firstly splits the mix into perpetually relevant frequency bands, secondly describes the \enquote{amount of panning} present in each band, and optionally estimates the number of sources present in the mix.

For a more detailed inspection of precisely where in the horizontal plane musical energy is located, one can instead utilize a \gls{ph} using the techniques described by \citeauthorandnumber{sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008} or \citeauthorandnumber{gomezMusicSimilaritySystems2008}.

While the aforementioned studies focus on descriptions of amplitude panning in stereo mixes, recording engineers can make use of other techniques such as to add spatial feeling to their mix. These techniques (described by \citeauthor{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011} as \enquote{Phase Stereo}) can be quantified using their definition of the \gls{ssps} \cite{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011}. In the same manuscript another method of quantifying amplitude panning is defined, which is based on the \gls{ild} of a cochleagram, and aims to contain more precision in the frequency axis than the azimuthal axis.

An alternative signal model is proposed by \citeauthor{harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2010} \cite{harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2010,harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2011,harmaStereoAudioClassification2011}, whereby a stereo mix is composed of combinations of centre, panned, and uncorrelated sources. Their subsequent classification of stereo field differentiates between different combinations of those source types.

\fxerror{Something about quality prediction?}

All the above use classical \gls{dsp} to create their spatial descriptors, but more recently there have been efforts to use \gls{dl} to disentangle spatial information from audio content. One such example was demonstrated by \citeauthorandnumber{yangUpmixingStyleTransfer2022}. While the original aim was to upmix stereo music to multichannel, a necessary component of the resulting neural network was a latent space that represents the spatial information of the multichannel signal. \gls{dl} has also been used outside the music domain to e.g. learn spatial relationships between individual microphones in an array \cite{yangSelfSupervisedLearningSpatial2024}. While such tasks do not directly address the research questions for this thesis, they demonstrate that learning spatial features on large amounts of unlabelled audio data is possible given appropriate methods and pretext task.

Neural generation of spatial audio continues to be a hot topic up to the present day, with many attempts to generate high-quality binaural, ambisonic or multichannel audio from mono sources \cite{limEnhancingSpatialAudio2024,liCyclicLearningBinaural2024,liTASPersonalizedTextguided2024,chenCCStereoAudioVisualContextual2025,melechovskySonicMasterControllableAllinOne2025,santosLargeScaleSelfSupervisedLearning2025,wangSpatialV2AVisualGuidedHighfidelity2026}. In doing so, the networks must infer spatial information from a source other than audio (typically an accompanying video or text prompt), and in the process will learn the spatial qualities of their audio training data.

The research was initiated with a period of exploration and refinement of the research questions. Two work streams (named Auxtrument and Orkester) were established early on as frameworks to explore the importance of spatial information in musical audio, firstly from a musical performance perspective, and secondly from a signal processing perspective. 

The Auxtrument initiative consisted of a joint project with many other researchers to make a musical performance that explored the use of space and merged realities. A custom instrument (the Auxtrument) was designed and built, the purpose of which was to generate a networked artificial reverberation chamber, embodying local sound sources with new spatial qualities \cite{franklinMergingPlacesRealTime2024,franklinComposingNetworkedReverberation2025}. This resulted in the retrospective paper, named \q{paper A} in this thesis \cite{franklinDevelopmentWebBasedRealTime2025}.

Running concurrently to Auxtrument, the \q{Orkester} initiative was an exploration of the more technical side of stereo field description. It involved a limited literature search to find methods of describing stereo field, followed by a period of software development to implement the discovered methods. Once the implementation was reliable, a period of musical exploration commenced, testing the various algorithms on recordings that were known to contain interesting spatial features. This had several outcomes. The most direct consequence was the inception of an objective experiment to determine how the various stereo field description techniques reacted to different musical recordings, which culminated in paper B \cite{miticObjectiveComparisonPanning2025}. 

Concurrently to the objective experiment, a perceptual listening test was planned and executed to determine how listeners react to the panning position of speech overlayed on top of music. The software developed for Orkester was subsequently used to analyse  the audio stimuli from the perceptual test in order to draw conclusions in paper C, which described the results and analysis of the perceptual test \cite{miticPerceptualEvaluationBinaurally2026}.

Lastly, through the developement of Orkester it became apparent that the original literature search was far from comprehensive. This spurred paper D, which was a systematic literature review designed to find all existing techniques that could be used to create a \gls{sip} \cite{miticSystematicLiteratureReview2026}.  

The combination of the exploratory work, objective experiment, and perceptual test provided a satisfactory answer for RQ\ref{rq:usecase}. The initial literature search plus the \gls{slr} provided an answer to RQ\ref{rq:methods}.

Adoption of MPEG-H and its branded version \enquote{Sony 360} is even lower. \enquote{DTS:X} is another proprietary spatial audio format, although its usage is generally limited to films released on physical media \cite{publishedDTSWhatIt2021}. Within the domain of spatial audio generation, \gls{vr} and \gls{ar} are the most common use cases given, although they are also yet to find mainstream adoption.
