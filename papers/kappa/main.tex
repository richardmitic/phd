\abstractdummy{
\begin{abstract}
Spatial information is crucial to the listener's experience of music, and stereophonic recordings have long been the standard for capturing this spatiality. Recent advancements in computing, coupled with the availability of new, very large musical corpora, have created opportunities for large-scale quantitative analysis of the stereo image of recorded music. In this licentiate thesis I address a key gap in current research: direct analysis of raw music recordings to describe the stereo image. I explore existing methods, assess their effectiveness, and demonstrate potential use cases for spatial analysis within audio reproduction, transmission and musical performance.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

It is a widely accepted truth in the world of music production and recording that spatial information in audio is crucial to  listeners' sense of immersion and connection with the music. To capture that spatiality, stereophonic recording and reproduction has been the standard format since it gained popularity in the 1950s \cite{lanierHIFITWOCHANNELCOMMOTION1957}. The spatial analysis of subsequent decades worth of stereo recordings presents a unique opportunity to study human musicality, as each recording reflects and encodes the creative choices made by the musicians and recordists involved.

Within the music community it is common to speak about spatiality in a qualitative sense, using terms such as \enquote{stereo image} and \enquote{stereo field} \footnote{The terms \q{stereo image} and \q{stereo field} are freely interchangeable and, as is normal in the music production community, I use them both throughout this thesis.} to encompass all audible spatial aspects. In academia however -- and specifically in the \gls{mir} community -- there have been several explicit attempts to translate these discussions into a quantitive domain \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007,sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008,tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011}. All were performed in an era when musical datasets were hand-curated/-labelled, fairly small (1,000 - 2,000 tracks), and, most notably, private. In recent years new musical corpora have become available, e.g. MTG-Jamendo \cite{bogdanovMTGJamendoDatasetAutomatic2019} and \gls{fma} \cite{defferrardFMADatasetMusic2017}, which are both orders of magnitude larger than their predecessors, containing approximately 55,000 and 100,000 tracks respectively. In addition, computing techniques in \gls{ml} and \gls{dl} have progressed to the point where it is now possible to learn extremely high-level features on these vast datasets, thereby making sense of the human creativity represented in the recordings. 

A typical use case for spatial descriptors that has emerged both in academia and industry is classification, and in particular, auto-tagging of musical genre/performer \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningInformation2010,ziemerGoniometersArePowerful2023}. Such features can be used in music similarity and recommendation tasks \cite{gomezMusicSimilaritySystems2008,ziemerNovelRecordingStudio2020}. It has also been shown that stereo field classification of subsections within a larger work can act as a precursor to other tasks such as segmentation, audio enhancement, upmixing, and source separation \cite{barryMusicStructureSegmentation2007,harmaStereoAudioClassification2011,harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2011}. The latter is very relevant to the audio distribution industry, which is currently experimenting with newer audio formats such as ambisonic and object-based audio. While these new formats offer exciting opportunities for both distributors and listeners, there remains a huge back-catalogue of stereophonic recordings that still need to be playable on modern reproduction equipment. A detailed  understanding of their spatial content would therefore be beneficial.

Historically, binaural audio (which is a subclass of stereo) has been of particular interest because its relevance to human hearing. Thus, it is extremely widely studied, and the underlying fundamental techniques are well-established \cite{blauertTechnologyBinauralListening2013}.

Yet there remains a gap in current research regarding direct analysis of raw music recordings, and in particular, describing the stereo image of said recordings at a high level. Through the work described in this thesis, I aim to fill that gap. \fxnote{I firstly demonstrate use cases where the spatial analysis of stereo audio has the opportunity to solve real-world problems in audio transmission, dissemination, and reproduction. Accordingly, I gather existing methods to describe stereo image and assess their abilities to detect the spatial aspects of a variety of musical audio sources.}

\chapter{Research Questions and Contributions}
\label{sec:research_questions_and_contributions}

The underlying purpose of the research documented in this thesis relates to \gls{mir}, and in particular to generating an understanding of the links between human musical creativity and musical output. While it might be possible for the creative people involved to explain how they make their music, it is far more objective to analyse their output as a representation of their musical ideas.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research_questions}

Given the aforementioned context, the overarching \gls{rq} can be formed as:

\vspace{1em}
\noindent \hspace{4em} \textit{How do music producers use stereo field?}
\vspace{1em}

The basis of the research is access to extremely large musical corpora and modern computing techniques. The number and variety of recordings available for analysis makes them a decent representation of music producers in general. The spatial aspects of each track can be analysed individually using traditional \gls{dsp} techniques and a population-level analysis can be performed on the spatial features of all tracks together. Thus, I have broken down the main \gls{rq} into two smaller ones:

\vspace{1em}
\begin{enumerate}[
    leftmargin=4em,
    rightmargin=4em,
    itemsep=0.618em
]
\item[$RQ_1$:] \textit{How does the stereo image of a recording relate to real-world musical knowledge?} \label{rq:usecase}
\item[$RQ_2$:] \textit{What methods can be used to describe the stereo image of a music recording?} \label{rq:methods}
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{1em}

These concrete questions are addressed through the four papers included in this licentiate thesis. \rqa~provides an important context of what quantitive spatial aspects receive attention from music practitioners, while \rqb~establishes a state-of-the-art baseline of relationships between those quantitive descriptions and objective measurements. The overarching research question spans both licentiate and doctoral studies, with future plans being detailed in \cref{sec:future_work}.


\section{Research contributions}
This section presents a summary of the research contributions achieved by the 4 included papers and explains their relationship to the \glspl{rq}. Many parts of the research required spatial descriptors to be extracted from raw audio signals. To facilitate this I implemented a library of software tools, allowing all the research tracks to be linked by a common mathematical framework.

\subsection{An understanding of the musical uses of stereo field}
With respect to the overarching research question, the act of personally creating musical output gives an insight into how music producers in general might use stereo field. We initially embarked on such a project, using audio from a remotely-located stereo microphone as the primary sonic material in a set of musical performances. Paper \ref{paper:auxtrument} was a retrospective account of the process, providing technical information to any musicians wishing to make a similar performance in the future. The paper also provided suggestions for future iterations of similar instruments and the standardized \glspl{api} which are built into web browsers, giving a basis for future work.

The software library presented the opportunity to apply a more technical approach to \rqa. The exploration of stereo field in existing recordings furthered the knowledge gained from an individual musical project by allowing us to analytically compare stereo fields created by many producers. This allowed us to establish an understanding of the range and variation that stereo field can embody.

\subsection{Signal processing techniques relating to stereo field}
The software implementation and the resulting paper \ref{paper:dafx} contribute an understanding of how some established techniques for measuring stereo field react to existing recorded music. Moreover, that understanding is not limited to the techniques specified in paper \ref{paper:dafx} since I developed the software continuously throughout several literature searches, resulting a full-featured library that includes many other spatial analysis techniques.

In order to fully answer \rqb, an extensive \gls{slr} (paper \ref{paper:slr}) was performed with two intentions. First and foremost, the intention was to conclusively find all existing mathematical techniques that describe stereo field. In addition, there was a goal to find \q{cross-pollination} opportunities from disparate areas of audio research which nonetheless derive spatial cues from audio signals. In doing so, it was possible to form a \q{general model} of \glspl{sip}, thereby making it easier to for future researchers to find or create a \gls{sip} that suits their specific use case.

\subsection{Psychoacoustics of mixed music and speech}
The perceptual listening test described in paper \ref{paper:bth} provided a new perspective on a classic problem in psychoacoustics research. While there is extensive literature describing how people perceive music or speech that has been intentionally masked by another sound, our experiment used music and speech that were intended to complement each other. The results firstly suggested that while people prefer speech that is panned towards the centre, there was an opportunity to increase the audible pleasantness by using very subtle panning, and moreover that said panning might be automated by analysis of the stereo field (thereby helping to answer \rqa). Furthermore, it became clear that the intelligibility of speech was paramount to pleasantness, despite the original assumption that the music and speech complemented each other.

\subsection{Setup for future work on large datasets of audio}
In regard to the future work described in \cref{sec:future_work}, the \gls{slr} provided both a list of relevant audio datasets,  and a many standardized implementations of mathematical techniques that extract spatial information from audio.
Paper \ref{paper:dafx} also presented a good opportunity to assess the computational complexity of the algorithms implemented in the software library, thereby highlighting some opportunities for optimization when they are applied to extremely large corpora.


\chapter{Background}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter provides the fundamental background knowledge that underpins the work included in the thesis. 

Music production techniques related to stereo field are thoroughly rooted in the human perception of sounds, exploiting well-understood auditory phenomena for creative means. However, that understanding is a fairly recent development. In fact, musical practice involving stereophonic sound predated the scientific knowledge of perception by several decades, with one of the earliest known examples dating back the late 19th century \cite{munnTelephoneParisOpera1881}. It wasn't until mid 20th century that the work of \citeauthorandnumber{fletcherLoudnessItsDefinition1933} and \citeauthorandnumber{jeffressPlaceTheorySound1948} outlined the psychoacoustic principles that music production would eventually exploit. In this chapter we draw a line from the basic human physiology of the auditory system, through psychoacoustics, \gls{dsp} theory, and music production, eventually linking them all to the modern computing techniques used as the basis for the work detailed in this thesis. 

\section{The mechanics of hearing and auditory perception}
\label{sec:hearing}

The physical characteristics of the human auditory system govern what we as humans are able to perceive. In particular, our perception of frequency, loudness, and directionality are key when assessing stereo field. 

% \citeauthor{lindsayHumanInformationProcessing1972}'s book \citetitle{lindsayHumanInformationProcessing1972} \cite{lindsayHumanInformationProcessing1972} gives a detailed description of how the human ear is constructed and how it enables us to hear. The relevant parts are summarized below.

\subsection{Physiology of the inner ear}
\label{sec:inner_ear}

The ear contains three major sections, loosely labelled the \q{outer}, \q{middle}, and \q{inner} ear (see \cref{fig:ear}). When air vibrations (i.e. sound waves) flow into the outer ear, mechanical coupling in the middle ear transmits vibrations to the cochlea -- the major organ of the inner ear. The cochlea is a conical tube which is coiled up into a spiral, filled with an incompressible fluid, and covered on either end by a membrane. Through the centre of the tube runs the basilar membrane, which is a covered in hairs named cilia. When vibrations are mechanically transmitted to the base of the cochlea, the internal fluid responds by transmitting those vibrations to the basilar membrane, and the cilia will bend and twist accordingly. The cilia are linked to nerve fibres, and will initiate electrical pulses when manipulated. These electrical pulses are sent to the brain and interpreted as sounds \cite{lindsayHumanInformationProcessing1972}.

The basilar membrane reacts to a pressure wave from an incoming sound in an elastic way, with a buldge travelling from the base (the widest section) to the apex (the narrowest section). That buldge will change amplitude as it travels due to the basilar membrane's non-uniform width and stiffness, and the distance at which is reaches its maximum amplitude depends on the frequency of the incoming sound. Thus, the cilia at different positions along the cochlea have different \q{critical frequencies} that they respond to. The critical frequency increases logarithmically as one moves from the base to the apex, as shown in \cref{fig:cochlea}. The cochlea therefore acts to convert time-domain sound waves into time- and frequency-dependent electrical excitations, enabling us to perceive different frequencies simultaneously.

\subsection{Directional hearing}
\label{sec:directional_hearing}
\Cref{sec:inner_ear} explains how sound waves are translated in to electrical impulses inside a single ear. When this mechanism is expanded to both ears, combinations of the two sets of electrical impulses can be combined to determine the direction of an incoming sound. 

The psychoacoustic study of spatial hearing has been an active area of research since the mid 20th century, with early examples including the Jeffress model in which time differences between sound events at each ear contribute to a person's ability to locate sounds in space \cite{jeffressPlaceTheorySound1948}. The work of \citeauthor{lindemannEvaluationInterauralSignal1982} a few decades later expanded on this idea by translating the earlier theory to a \gls{dsp} context, and establishing the \gls{ild}, \gls{itd}, and \gls{iacc} as important measures to describe the spatial aspects of binaural signals in a quantifiable way \cite{lindemannEvaluationInterauralSignal1982}.

Put simply, we are able to perceive the incoming direction of a sound by observing both the amplitude difference and time difference of coincident sound waves arriving at the left and right ears. Figure \cref{fig:directional_hearing} shows how these two properties enable directional hearing. Firstly, the head itself creates a physical barrier that attenuates sound waves at one ear more-so that at the other ear. Secondly, the difference in distance between the sound source and each ear will create a time delay between the received signals. Over time we learn to associate patterns in \gls{itd}/\gls{ild} with the physical location of the sound source.

The relative importance of \gls{ild} and \gls{itd} differ depending on the frequency of the incoming sound. The reasons for this are twofold. Firstly, a person's inter-aural distance (the physical distance between their ears) must be longer than the wavelength of the incoming sound wave in order for them to detect the phase difference between wave peaks. In this case, it is often referred to as \gls{ipd}, which accounts for the centre frequency of the critical band. Secondly the cilia that line the basilar membrane inside the cochlea can only detect the fine structure of a sound wave up until roughly \qty{1.6}{\kHz} \cite{lindemannEvaluationInterauralSignal1982}. \citeauthor{lindemannEvaluationInterauralSignal1982} gives a rule of thumb that time differences of less than \qty{7.5}{\ms} are heard as a single auditory event (for which direction could be inferred), and differences above \qty{21}{\ms} are heard as uncorrelated events \cite{lindemannEvaluationInterauralSignal1982}. Hence, at \qty{1.6}{\kHz} and below \gls{itd} is more important, whereas above \qty{1.6}{\kHz} \gls{ild} is more important. Furthermore, \citeauthor{blauertSpatialHearingOne1996}'s subsequent work found firstly that both \gls{ild} and \gls{itd} are relevant within the frequency band surrounding \qty{1.6}{\kHz}, and secondly that the angle of incidence of the sound wave plays a role in how the cues are perceived \cite{blauertSpatialHearingOne1996}. Despite this, Lindemann's rules of thumb are still broadly accepted, even being encoded into international standards such as \cite{iso-11172-3:mpeg_audio}. As an example, \cref{fig:example_pan} shows two recordings (a high-frequency hi-hat and a low-frequency kick drum) that originate from the right-hand side of a listener. The high-frequency hi-hat has a large \gls{ild} and ambiguous \gls{ipd}, whereas the low-frequency kick drum has negligible \gls{ild}, but an interpretable \gls{ipd}.

\subsection{Loudness perception}
While we have established that the \gls{ild} is a key factor of directional hearing, a more detailed understanding of the link between perceived loudness and sound wave intensity is necessary for musical applications.

We perceive the loudness of sound, not in terms of the absolute amplitude of the sound wave, but the \textit{power} of the sound wave. Mathematically speaking, perceived loudness is proportional to the \gls{rms} \gls{spl} raised to an exponent \cite{mooreIntroductionPsychologyHearing2013}. Observing that the calculation of \gls{rms} naturally involves taking the square of the original time function (see \cref{eq:rms_cont,eq:rms_disc} for the continuous and discrete forms respectively), it follows that multiplication of a signal by $\sqrt{2}$ will result in double perceived loudness. Conversely, multiplying a signal by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ will halve the perceived loudness.

\begin{align}
    f_{RMS}(t) &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{T_2 - T_1} \int_{T_1}^{T_2}[f(t)^2]dt} \label{eq:rms_cont}\\
    f_{RMS}[n] &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N}f[n]^2} \label{eq:rms_disc}
\end{align}

This basic rule is relevant when two sounds are mixed in music production. It defines a precise, mathematical relationship between perceived loudness and the amplification applied by the production equipment or software.

\section{Music production}
\label{sec:music_production}

The principles of our auditory perception have been exploited for creative use since the turn of the 20th century. Early stereophony came from the world of cinema, with sound technicians adding multiple speakers to enable spatial effects\footnote{A typical use case was to match the origin of a voice with the actor's location on the screen.}. Eventually migrating into the music industry, stereophonic recording became the norm in the 1950s \cite{lanierHIFITWOCHANNELCOMMOTION1957,izhakiPanning2023,AbbeyRoadOurStory}. While both cinema and music practitioners have long known that using two speakers for reproduction enabled spatial effects, knowledge of how to \textit{control} those effects came with the psychoacoustic understanding detailed in \cref{sec:hearing}. 


\subsection{Panning}
\label{sec:panning}
The concepts of directional hearing are applied in music production to make sound sources appear as though they originate from a specific direction or position, often referred to as the \q{phantom position}. In recording studio vernacular, this is call \q{panning}. In accordance with the two signal differences a that humans are able to detect (\gls{ild} and \gls{itd}), there are also two basic types of panning -- amplitude panning and time panning. Time panning (otherwise known as the \q{Haas effect}) exploits the phenomenon that the first ear to hear a sound generally dictates the direction that it appeared to come from. The process is to simply delay the signal in one channel by a time value within the \q{Haas window} (typically less than 40ms). The un-delayed channel will then dictate the phantom direction of the original source. In music production it is used both for panning and to add spaciousness to a recording \cite{stewartWhatHaasEffect2022,izhakiPanning2023}. 

Another frequently used technique is \q{binaural panning}, which assigns combinations of amplitude and time difference to each stereo channel. This is typically achieved by using a \gls{hrir}. An \gls{hrir} is a pair of impulse responses recorded with microphones placed in the ears of either a real person or a dummy head. To obtain an \gls{hrir}, a known excitation signal is played from a specific position in a room and recorded with said binaural microphones (see \cref{fig:phantom_binaural}). The \gls{hrir} can then be convolved with any other monophonic recording to make it appear as though the recording came from the position of the excitation source. An \gls{hrir} can only be used to pan a sound to a single position, so it is typical to record many from different positions \cite{armstrongPerceptualEvaluationIndividual2018}.

In traditional recording studio setups, the recording engineer or producer will use one or more microphones to capture an original sound source. These singular sound sources are commonly called \q{stems}. During the production process, the stems may be panned to create a sense of space in the final mix. In the case that the stem was recorded using two microphones, the stem would be called a \q{stereo stem}, and the term \q{panning} is usually replaced by \q{balance}, and only amplitude panning (as opposed to time or binaural panning) would be applied.

Amplitude panning is the most common panning technique in traditional music production \cite{shelvockHearingYourAudience2020}. It is associated with so-called \q{panning laws}, which govern how the amplitude is changed in each channel according the desired pan position \cite{izhakiPanning2023}. One example is the \q{energy-preserving pan law} (sometimes called the \mbox{\q{-3dB pan law}}) in which the squared sum of the channel gains always equals 1. The gains of the left and right channel are calculated by $g_L=\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}a)$ and $g_R=\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}a)$ respectively where $a$ is the panning position in the range $[0,1]$. \Cref{fig:equal_power_panning} shows how the channel gains relate to the panning position. The total perceived loudness will be equal at all panning positions.

Panning techniques are applied to single stems only. A typical recording session will contain multiple stems, each with its own panning.

\subsection{Stereo image}
The term \q{stereo image} is used to describe the overall distribution of sound across the horizontal plane after panning and any other production techniques have been applied on all stems. One may also use terms like \q{stereo field}, \q{soundstage}, or \q{panorama}, which all refer to the same thing. They are usually described in qualitative terms such as \q{big}, \q{wide}, and \q{ambient} rather than quantitative measures \cite{owsinskiMixingEngineersHandbook2006}. When mixing a musical recording, a producer will pan individual stems to their desired position within the stereo image. Those stems which are recorded in stereo in fact have their own stereo image, which can also be controlled by the producer. The combination of all stems into a final stereo mix, constitutes the overall stereo image. 

\Cref{fig:stereo_image} shows a simple example stereo image, as perceived by the producer. It contains two mono stems -- vocals and violin, and one stereo stem -- piano. There are some common tropes seen throughout many productions, some of which are shown in the diagram. Firstly, vocals are recorded in mono and panned to the centre. Secondly, since the piano is panned slightly left, the violin is panned roughly the same amount to the right in order to achieve a balanced mix. Thirdly, all stems are placed in the stereo image in a way that emulates a natural performance on a stage, and to avoid separate instruments masking each other. Note that while these practices are commonplace, they are purely an artistic choice on behalf of the producer \cite{owsinskiMixingEngineersHandbook2006}; Recordings breaking the trend are not difficult to find.

Describing the stereo image of a recording has its roots in the acoustics domain, in which typical use cases are the analysis of concert halls and music reproduction systems. In these cases, the previously mentioned spatial cues (\gls{ild}, \gls{itd}, and \gls{iacc}) typically form the basis of the analysis. \Citeauthor{bergSystematicEvaluationPerceived2003}  give a long list of typical perceptual measures that can be derived from the low-level cues \cite{bergSystematicEvaluationPerceived2003}, although later literature has focused on the measures of \gls{asw} and \gls{lev}. However, as stated by \citeauthor{sarroffComputationalModelPerceived2011}, the task of describing the spatial aspects of recorded music is inherently different to that of acoustical signals since said recordings may exhibit spatial aspects that cannot occur in physical acoustic space \cite{sarroffComputationalModelPerceived2011}.


\subsection{Production equipment and software}

To create a stereo image, a music producer will use the equipment available in a typical recording studio. Before the advent of digital recording, said equipment usually comprised a mixing console where each stem was assigned to one \q{track}. Each track had individual controls for (amongst others) level and panning that were directly linked to the internal electronics. The producers might also use the term \q{fader} for the level control and \q{pan-pot} for the panning control\footnote{The term \q{pan-pot} is a shortening of \q{panoramic potentiometer}.}. All tracks would be mixed by the console and the combined output fed to stereo speakers for playback. In a digital recording studio, the equipment is largely the same except that the mixing console will be connected to a computer that controls the session and stores the recorded audio. \cref{fig:recording_studio} shows photographs of a small recording studio where the faders and pan-pots for each track are visible. The music producer will manipulate the physical controls for each track to create a stereo mix.

The process of creating a stereo image is largely a subjective one. The producer pans each track individually by either physically turning the \mbox{pan-pots} or adjusting a digital panning control, and judges the stereo image purely by listening. In a large \gls{daw} session containing many tracks, the producer will make artistic choices about where in the stereo field to pan each track. In some cases the producer consults monitoring equipment such as a level meter or goniometer to fine tune their mix \cite{ziemerAcousticFeaturesRecording2021,ziemerGoniometersArePowerful2023}. \subsection{Audio representations}
Audio representations are standard paradigms that an audio recording can follow. If we consider an audio recording as a set of concurrent time signals, the representation defines how each signal in the set should be interpreted. They are usually grouped into \q{channel-based}, \q{scene-based}, and \q{object-based}. In channel-based audio, each signal represents the exact waveform that should be played back by a single loudspeaker. In scene-based and object-based audio, each signal represents one part of a larger scene, and rendering equipment is responsible for translating that scene to individual speakers \cite{sugimotoSpecificationAudioRepresentations2024}. While the focus of this thesis in on stereo audio, it is important to compare stereo to the wider audio landscape to understand why it is so heavily used for recorded music.
% The term \q{spatial audio} is quite contested in the literature, but this thesis uses \citeauthor{cobosOverviewMachineLearning2022}'s definition from \cite{cobosOverviewMachineLearning2022}: \q{audio content that contains spatial information}. 

\subsubsection*{Monophonic}
Monophonic recordings contain a single channel to be played back on one speaker. They are only able to convey a small amount of spatial information; reverberation is the only way to infer a sense of space. Archival/historical recordings will be recorded in mono, as well as a majority of raw studio stems and most speech/telephony recordings.

\subsubsection*{Stereophonic}
Stereo recordings contain two channels: left and right. Compared to mono, stereo has the ability to convey the width and location of a sound 2D space. For music this sense of space is especially important since music signals tend to be highly dense and complex. Stereo mixing therefore allows music producers to add clarity and balance to their recordings. This marked increase in quality drove stereo's commercial adoption in the 1950s, and it has been the dominant format for recorded music since then \cite{lanierHIFITWOCHANNELCOMMOTION1957}.

\subsubsection*{Binaural}
Binaural can be considered a subset of stereo. Each channel conveys the precise sound wave as heard at the left and right ears. It is typically used when rendering spatial audio\footnote{The exact definition of \q{spatial audio} is somewhat contested in the literature but here I assume \citeauthor{cobosOverviewMachineLearning2022}'s definition: \textit{\q{audio that contains spatial information}} \cite{cobosOverviewMachineLearning2022}.} for playback on headphones, and in \gls{vr} / \gls{ar} scenarios.

\subsubsection*{Multichannel}
Multichannel is a continuation of stereo which adds speakers at more positions. While it could contain any number of channels, there are some standards that are referred to by their channel count and layout, e.g. 5.1 (5 listener-plane channels, 1 \gls{lfe} channel) or 22.2 (10 listener-plane, 2 \gls{lfe}, 9 overhead, 3 low) \cite{sugimotoSpecificationAudioRepresentations2024}. Compared to stereo, it has the ability to convey width, depth, and -- depending on the arrangement -- height, creating a higher sense of immersion. This ability sees it being used mainly for film and TV soundtracks where the emotional impact of a sound is critical. That said, the high setup cost and difficulty positioning extra speakers has historically discouraged its wider adoption \cite{bayleySurroundSoundDAW2012}.

\subsubsection*{Ambisonics}
Ambisonics is a scene-based audio representation where each channel is one directional component of a 3D physical sound field. Recordings are referred to by their \q{order}, which implies both the number of different directions and the number of channels. The number of required channels increases quadratically with the order according to the formula $C=(O + 1)^2$ where $C$ is the number of channels and $O$ is the order. Ambisonics typically excels at representing diffuse sounds, but struggle to represent point sources unless the order is very high. Thus, ambisonic recordings are only used for very specific types of music, such as orchestral performances in large concert halls where accurate recreation of the ambience is more important than individual instruments. Even in those cases, an ambisonic recording will generally be rendered to binaural for mass distribution \cite{churnsideAmbisonicsPeriphony2010}.

\subsubsection*{Object-based audio}
In object-based audio, the entire audio scene is composed of individual audio objects accompanied by metadata specifying their location and movement. Rendering equipment is then responsible for translating those objects to speaker channels for playback. It is a relatively new format and while its main use is for cinematic sound, the music industry has recently begun to adopt it. In contrast to ambisonic recordings, object-based audio is distributed in its original form, allowing the same set of signals to be rendered in multiple ways.
\\\\
Overall, stereo recordings are able to deliver high enough quality for the majority of music listeners. The newer formats, while gathering interest in pockets of the music community, have yet to receive wide-spread adoption from consumers. The result is that the mainstream distribution of music still happens in stereo.

\subsection{Lossless and lossy encoding}
Regardless of which audio representation is chosen, the resulting recording will most likely be encoded to a compressed format for distribution. There are two types of compression: \q{lossless} and \q{lossy}. A lossless format, when decoded, will produce bit-for-bit recreation of the input signal. Lossy formats on the other hand will not produce the exact same signal, but will discard information that they consider irrelevant according to a psychoacoustic model. There are many lossy formats each suitable for different types of audio, but for the purposes of this thesis it is just pertinent that spatial information can be lost during lossy encoding.

\section{Analysis of digital recordings}

The output of a recording session is usually only the stereo mix. Stems and \gls{daw} sessions are generally kept private, with precious few being available under licensing terms that are conducive to academic research \cite{bittnerMedleyDBMultitrackDataset2014}. Thus, we assume that all analysis can only be performed on the final stereo mixes that are distributed for playback. The most common distribution format is a digital audio file, and there are some fundamental analysis techniques that can be applied to a digital audio signal.

\subsection{Digital audio signals}
When a sound wave interacts with a microphone, it generates an electrical signal in continuous time. To convert that analogue signal into a digital one, it is sampled at regular intervals. The sampling frequency ($f_s$) is related to the sampling interval ($\Delta$) by the relationship $\Delta = \frac{1}{f_s}$.

Shannon's sampling theorem states that a signal can be perfectly recreated when sampled at twice the highest frequency contained in the signal. Conversely, the highest frequency that can be represented by sampling a signal at a given $f_s$ is half of that frequency. This limit is called the \q{Nyquist frequency} and is calculated by $f_N = \frac{f_s}{2}$ \cite{yaoNyquistFrequency1983}. This is illustrated by \Cref{fig:nyquist}, which shows the sampling of three waveforms. The lower plot shows a signal at the Nyquist frequency ($f_N$), which can be perfectly reconstructed from the digital samples. The upper two waveforms have frequencies equidistant but in opposite directions from the Nyquist frequency ($f_N \pm f_{\delta}$). The digital samples shown in circles could represent either of the two waveforms. In practice, when the analogue waveform is reconstructed, the lower frequency will always take precedence. 

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/nyquist.pdf}
    \caption{Aliasing in sampled digital audio signals. Dotted vertical lines represent digital sampling points with a separation of $T_s$, and monotonically increasing index $n$. The upper plot shows two cosine waves with frequencies equidistant from the Nyquist frequency ($f_N$) in opposing directions. The lower plot is a cosine wave at exactly $f_N$. Sampled values are shown as circle marks on the plots. The two upper signals can both be represented by same digital samples.}
    \label{fig:nyquist}
\end{figure}

A typical $f_s$ for music is \qty{44.1}{\kHz}, with a resulting $f_N$ of \qty{22.05}{\kHz}. The prevalence of this particular number is due to the popularity of \glspl{cd} as a distribution medium\footnote{\qty{44.1}{\kHz} was chosen by a consortium of \gls{cd} manufacturing companies as a trade-off between technical requirements and the limits of human perception \cite{baertDigitalAudioCompact2013}.}. While higher frequencies may be used in the recording and production process, an exceedingly large proportion of recorded music has been distributed at \qty{44.1}{\kHz}.

\subsection{Time-Frequency representation of audio}

In order to perform many types of audio analysis, the audio signal can be converted from the time domain into the \gls{tf} domain. In perceptual acoustics research there are several common forms of \gls{tf} representation, each with its own nuances. This section provides those details and \Cref{fig:spect_comp} shows a visual comparison of all spectrogram types.

\subsubsection{\gls{stft} spectrogram}

The \gls{stft} spectrogram is one of the most popular \gls{tf} representions across all audio processing work due to its computational efficiency. The fundamental principle is that a time-domain signal is split into short (1 to 100ms) overlapping chunks, and the \gls{fft} is performed on each chunk, thereby converting that chunk into the frequency domain.  By arranging subsequent frames along a time axis it is possible to represent the original signal as a 2D image known as a spectrogram. When the time-domain signal is entirely real, (which is the case for monophonic audio signals), the result of the \gls{fft} will be a complex signal which is symmetrical around the Nyquist frequency and the upper half is therefore dropped. Furthermore, the complex spectrum is usually represented in magnitude/phase form to better match the human auditory response.

A fundamental property of the \gls{fft} is that it assumes that the signal repeats outside the time frame that is being analysed. Since this is generally not the case for audio signals, it is standard practice to apply a time-domain windowing function to each frame so that large transients do not occur at the boundaries. Serveral standard windowing functions exist, each with their own nuances, but for most audio applications the \q{Hann} window can be used \cite{LibrosastftLibrosa0110}. 

The nature of the spectrogram is affected by two parameters: the frame size and hop size. \q{Frame size} refers to the number of samples in each analysis frame. To obtain maximal computational efficiency this must be a power of 2. \q{Hop size} refers to the number of samples between the start of subsequent frames and is sometimes expressed as a percentage of the frame size. The frame size dictates the resolution of the spectrogram's frequency axis since the frequency bins will be spaced evenly between \SI{0}{\hertz} and the Nyquist frequency. Importantly, a longer frame size will result in lower time accuracy within each frame; sharp transients in the time domain will appear as wideband energy in the frequency domain, but information about exactly where the transient occurred within the frame is lost. To counteract this inaccuracy, a smaller hop size can be used so that transients might be detected as sharp changes between \gls{stft} frames. One major drawback of the \gls{stft} spectrogram is that the linearly-spaced frequency bins do not align well with the human perception of frequency, which is logarithmic. The \gls{stft} therefore contains redundant information in higher frequencies. Several common alternatives exist to account for this.

\subsubsection{Mel spectrogram}

The \q{Mel scale} is a quasi-logarithmic frequency scale derived from perceptual experiments. Several definitions exist in the literature \cite{umeshFittingMelScale1999}, but popular implementations like that of librosa \cite{mcfeeLibrosaAudioMusic2015} and \gls{amt} \cite{majdakAMT1xToolbox2022} tend to underpin most audio experimentation.

A \q{Mel filter bank} is a set of band-pass filters that are spaced equally on the Mel scale. Practically, they are implemented as sets of weights that are applied to an \gls{stft} spectrum with the relationship $M_n = \sum_{k=0}^{N} S_k W_{k,n}$ where $M_n$ is the $n$th Mel coefficient, $S_k$ is the $k$th \gls{stft} bin, $N$ is the number of \gls{stft} bins,  and $W_{k,n}$ is the $k$th weight of the $n$th Mel filter. An example Mel filter bank with 8 filters is shown in \cref{fig:mel_filters}. While 8 filters are shown for demonstration purposes, audio analysis typically requires over 100. 

When this filterbank is applied to each time step of an \gls{stft} spectrogram, the result is a \q{Mel spectrogram}, an example of which can be seen in \cref{fig:spect_comp}. Observe that the higher frequencies have been condensed into fewer frequency bins compared to the \gls{stft} spectrogram.

\subsubsection{Cochleagram}

As an alternative to the two spectrogram representations, a cochleagram aims to make a \gls{tf} representation that models the acoustic response of the human cochlea more closely. To that end, all processing is performed in the time domain and is sometimes referred to as a \q{peripheral ear model}. Several examples appear in the literature \cite{boddenBinauralModelingAuditory1995,choiObjectiveMeasurementPerceived2008,delgadoDesignChoicesBinaural2023}. In the classical method \cite{boddenBinauralModelingAuditory1995} the original signal is first processed by a bank of gammatone filters with their centre frequencies placed according to a perceptual scale (such as the \gls{erb} scale \cite{smithiiiEquivalentRectangularBandwidth2020}), as shown in \cref{fig:gammatone_filters}. As with Mel filters, several definitions of gammatone filters exist in the literature, but the popular implementations (e.g. scipy \cite{virtanenSciPy10Fundamental2020} and \gls{amt} \cite{majdakAMT1xToolbox2022}) preside.

The amplitude of each chunk is then calculated as the mean of all its values, forming one pixel in the cochleagram.

\subsection{Machine learning}
Any data-driven audio research, particularly that using vast datasets, will naturally involve the use of \gls{ml}. Broadly speaking, \gls{ml} techniques can be classified as either supervised or unsupervised. When training a supervised  \gls{ml} model, the training dataset needs to include ground-truth labels such that the network can objectively discern via a loss function whether the predictions it is making are correct. Unsupervised \gls{ml} on the other hand does not require any labels. Instead, it seeks to form a representation of the underlying dataset via techniques such as clustering or dimensionality reduction, thereby learning the structures and patterns of the data while retaining as much key information as possible.

Striking a middle ground between the two is \q{self-supervised} learning, which addresses the issue of creating objective ground-truths from unlabelled data. Instead of relying on external labels, self-supervised learning generates pseudo-labels from the dataset itself, and then uses those as the input to a \q{pretext task}, which is tackled in much the same way as a supervised task.

As an example we can consider the current largest dataset of recorded music: \gls{fma} \cite{defferrardFMADatasetMusic2017}. The dataset contains the stereo audio of approximately \num{100000} tracks, along with metadata such as genre tags. An unsupervised task might involve learning a high-order representation of the raw spatial cues present in the stereo signals. A supervised task might involve predicting genre using the audio signal as input. A self-supervised task might involve masking off a short period of audio and filling it back in using the surrounding audio as context. 

In relation to the \hyperref[sec:research_questions]{overarching \gls{rq}} in \cref{sec:research_questions}, we assume that a music producer's \gls{daw} session is the only source of objective ground-truth data regarding stereo field. Furthermore, we assume that \gls{daw} sessions are not publicly available, as was found in \cite{colonelReverseEngineeringRecording2021,rodriguezSpectralDynamicConstellations2024}; The only public-facing output of a recording session is the mixdown. It follows that either unsupervised or self-supervised learning is necessary to draw any conclusions to the \glspl{rq}.

\section{A brief history of stereo field in industry}
\label{sec:history}
Research into the stereo field of recorded music specifically (as opposed to generic spatial properties of audio signals) has its roots in the early 2000s. At the time, stereo recording was a well-established norm and audio distribution was handled by the early lossy encoding formats such as \gls{mp3} and \gls{aac} \cite{iso-11172-3:mpeg_audio,ISOIEC13818-7:2006-AAC}. Analysis and description of stereo field was used to increase the coding efficiency of these codecs, and the was eventually realized by e.g. \gls{ps} coding \cite{fallerParametricCodingSpatial2004,breebaartHighqualityParametricSpatial2004,breebaartParametricCodingStereo2005}. In parallel, new audio encoding schemes such as \gls{bcc} (later ratified as \q{\glsxtrshort{mpeg} Surround} \cite{iso/iec23003-1InformationTechnologyMPEG2007}) were developed based on the work of \citeauthorandnumber{baumgarteEstimationAuditorySpatial2002}. 

The standardization of multi-channel audio presented an opportunity: new reproduction equipment needed to remain compatible with the existing back-catalogue of stereo recordings, and vendors could compete on sound quality. There was therefore a need to invent algorithms that upmixed stereo audio to multi-channel. Analysis of the stereo field, source separation, and primary/ambient extraction were seen as an effective way to achieve this \cite{goodwinPrimaryAmbientSignalDecomposition2007}. In fact, such research is still ongoing today \cite{shiFastNonuniformSearching2022}.

In the prevailing years, object-based audio has been developed and standardized as e.g. Dolby Atmos \cite{DolbyAtmosNextGenerationAudioforCinema} and MPEG-H \cite{mpeg-h}, and the same early research into stereo field analysis is still relevant to these new formats for all the same reasons. Nowadays, the same tasks are also being tackled using \gls{ml}, and many of the new approaches out-perform their \gls{dsp}-based predecessors \cite{findsomething}.

With access to these efficient distribution formats, large digital music collections emerged and brought new problems to solve: collectors needed ways organize and categorize their music. Stereo field analysis was just one of the mechanisms used to facilitate this, paving the way for research into e.g. \gls{agr}, \gls{mir}, and music recommendation \cite{guausAudioContentProcessing2009,harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2010,defferrardFMADatasetMusic2017}.

\section{Spatial cues for stereo image}
\label{sec:spatial_cues}

A \q{spatial cue} is a low-level analysis of spatial information in a short (1-100ms) audio snippet. While those mentioned in \cref{sec:directional_hearing} aim to analyse binaural sound as perceived by humans, there are several examples of spatial cues tailored specifically to the analysis of recorded music.

The \enquote{\gls{pi}} and \enquote{ambience index} specified by \citeauthor{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002} \cite{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002} are loosely analogous to \gls{ild} and \gls{iacc} in the acoustics domain. To estimate the \gls{pi}, a signal model is assumed whereby a musical source is panned within the mix using the \enquote{energy preserving pan law}, in which amplitude differences are applied to each channel such that the sum of the squared amplitudes equals 1. While this panning technique does not affect the phase of either channel, existing phase differences between channels are measured using the \enquote{ambience index}, which is defined such that it is low when the stereo channels are correlated (indicating a direct sound source), and high when channels are uncorrelated (indicating an ambient or reverberant sound source). 

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/pi_ai.pdf}
    \caption{Demonstration of \q{panning index} and \q{ambience index} on Track C. Several panned sources are visible as red or blue bands in the \pani, and at roughly 20s most sources fade out leaving a single one on the right. The \ambi~plot shows that the track starts off with no single dominant direct sound source. At 20s when most sources fade out, the \ambi~drops to 0 across the lower half of the spectrum, indicating that the remaining source is direct. In the upper half of the spectrum, the source remains slightly ambient. }
    \label{fig:pi_ai}
\end{figure}

\citeauthor{prestiBivariateMixtureSpace2023} provides a re-contextualization of the panning and ambience indices in his work on \glspl{bms} \cite{prestiVisualizationManipulationStereophonic2014,prestiBivariateMixtureSpace2023}. He demonstrates that it can be used to analyse the panning position of individual \gls{tf} bins, plus the clarity (or trustworthyness) of that measurement. Thus, it is useful for tasks such as primary/ambient decomposition, stereo field manipulation, and visualization.

\citeauthor{barrySoundSourceSeparation2004} present another seminal technique in their paper defining the \gls{adress} algorithm \cite{barrySoundSourceSeparation2004}. The fundamental principle is to estimate the panning position of frequency bins in a stereo spectrogram by scaling one channel, inverting it, and mixing the channels back to mono. The resulting phase cancellation will determine the scaling factor that was applied to the original audio source during amplitude panning, and hence will show where the source was panned in the stereo field. To demonstrate, \cref{fig:adress} shows an example \gls{fa} plane for a combination of panned sine waves. While this was technique was originally intended to be used for audio source separation, it has been repurposed many times since.

\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.618\textwidth]{figures/adress.pdf}
    \caption{\glsxtrlong{fa} plane for a signal comprising 8 sinusoids at differing frequencies. Half are panned left and half are panned right.}
    \label{fig:adress}
\end{figure}

Importantly, all the previously mentioned analyses are performed in the \gls{tf} domain and result in one value per \gls{tf} bin. Their use cases typically involve reconstruction or editing of the original audio source as opposed to \textit{description} of it since they inherently entangle semantic content with spatial information. They have, however, become the basis of other algorithms that are designed specifically for description.

\section{Stereo image description}
\label{sec:stereo_image_description}

Stereo image description refers to the generation of a numerical vector that represents the stereo image in an arbitrary feature space. Recordings with similar stereo image should be close within the space, although the distance metric is application-specific. A typical technique is to construct the vector from statistical summaries of low-level spatial cues. Throughout this section, these vectors are referred to as \glspl{sip}, and examples of each one are given on the three example tracks.

\citeauthor{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007} provide the earliest example of a technique specifically designed to interpret the use of panning in recorded music: \gls{spf} \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007}. It targets amplitude panning only, using \citeauthor{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002}'s \pani{} as the basis. Additionally, it separates the frequency spectrum into 3 sub-bands -- lows, mids, and highs -- following the common practice of music producers. While not specifically stated by \citeauthor{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007}, I interpret the vector to represent the amount and variance of \q{fast panning} and \q{slow panning} in each frequency band. \citeauthor{tzanetakisStereoPanningInformation2010} later expanded the features by adding an estimation of the number of individual sources in the recording \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningInformation2010}. The resulting \gls{sip} can be used to compare the recording and production styles of different music tracks, as shown in \cref{fig:spf}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/spf.pdf}
    \caption{
        \glsxtrlong{spf} for three tracks from the \gls{fma} dataset \cite{defferrardFMADatasetMusic2017}. All tracks clearly show different panning and production styles. 
    }
    \label{fig:spf}
\end{figure}

While \gls{spf} shows production style at a high level, other descriptors contain more detail. \citeauthorandnumber{gomezMusicSimilaritySystems2008} and \citeauthorandnumber{sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008} both present separate methods to form a \gls{ph}, which displays the likelihood of a sound source being panned to a specific azimuth. \Cref{fig:panning_histograms} shows examples of both methods for the same three tracks as \cref{fig:spf}. As \citeauthor{sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008} state, the \q{azimuth} value should not be interpreted as a true angular position relative to the listener, but rather as a loose placement of a sound source within the stereo field. Note that compared to \gls{spf}, \gls{ph} visualizes the exact location of sound energy in the stereo field rather than just the \q{amount of panning}. On the other hand, \gls{ph} looses information about the \textit{dynamics} of the panning, which \gls{spf} does not. \citeauthorandnumber{sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008} subsequently use the \gls{ph} to calculate an estimate of stereo width, which is an important perceptual measure (see \cref{sec:prediction_of_perceptual_scalars}).

\citeauthor{gomezMusicSimilaritySystems2008} present a continuation of their \gls{ph} algorithm. They note that the histogram itself can be quite large, and that standard distance metrics may not correlate with perceptual differences. To avoid this, they propose converting the \gls{ph} to a vector of \q{\gls{pc}} in a non-perceptual domain and truncating it. \Cref{eq:cepst_ph} shows this conversion where $\vec{C}$ is the vector of coefficients, $\mathcal{F}^{-1}$ is the inverse \gls{fft}, $L$ is the truncation length, and $\vec{H}\vec{H}^{\leftarrow}$ is a concatenation of the panning histogram and its reverse. The efficacy of this technique remains under-tested, and my work in paper \ref{paper:dafx} showed that \gls{pc} was unable to accurately represent \glspl{ph} with sharp peaks.

\begin{equation}
    \vec{C} = \left\{\mathcal{F}^{-1} \left\{ \log (\vec{H}\vec{H}^{\leftarrow})\right\} \right\}_{1:L}
    \label{eq:cepst_ph}
\end{equation}


\citeauthor{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011} present another assessment of amplitude panning named \gls{asf} \cite{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011}. Differing from the previously mentioned techniques, \citeauthor{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011}'s method focuses on providing measurements of the amount and variation of panning in many frequency bands. The basis of the algorithm is the \gls{ild} of a stereo cochleagram, as opposed to an \gls{stft} spectrogram. \citeauthor{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011} use 70 frequency bands equidistant on the \gls{erb} scale, each of which produce multiple features. Thus, the resulting \gls{sip} vector is an order of magnitude larger than \gls{spf} or \gls{ph}. As \cref{fig:asf} shows, \gls{asf}'s large size allows for a very detailed assessment of panning across the frequency dimension, although it runs the risk of including noise and redundant information.

\gls{dl} has also been used outside the music domain to e.g. learn spatial relationships between individual microphones in an array \cite{yangSelfSupervisedLearningSpatial2024}. While such tasks do not directly address the research questions in this thesis, they demonstrate that learning spatial features on large amounts of unlabelled audio data is possible given appropriate methods and pretext task.

Neural generation of spatial audio continues to be a hot topic up to the present day, with many attempts to generate high-quality binaural, ambisonic or multichannel audio from mono sources \cite{limEnhancingSpatialAudio2024,liCyclicLearningBinaural2024,liTASPersonalizedTextguided2024,chenCCStereoAudioVisualContextual2025,melechovskySonicMasterControllableAllinOne2025,santosLargeScaleSelfSupervisedLearning2025,wangSpatialV2AVisualGuidedHighfidelity2026}. In doing so, the networks must infer spatial information from a source other than audio (typically an accompanying video or text prompt), and in the process will learn the spatial qualities of their audio training data.

\section{Prediction of perceptual scalars}
\label{sec:prediction_of_perceptual_scalars}
A perceptual scalar is a measurement that would usually be made via a perceptual listening test, e.g. \gls{asw} or \gls{lev} \cite{rumseySpatialAudioSensory2006}. Those tests are known to be slow and costly \cite{rumseySpatialQualityEvaluation2002}. Prediction of the perceptual scalars via objective means is therefore particularly useful to researchers as it allows for faster experimentation.

\gls{asw} is one of the more common values that has been addressed in the literature. The early work of \citeauthorandnumber{supperOnsetGuidedSpatialAnalyser2005}, \citeauthorandnumber{masonImplementationApplicationBinaural2006}, and \citeauthorandnumber{dewhirstModellingPerceivedSpatial2008} detail the construction of \q{binaural analysers} that are able to estimate \gls{asw}. \citeauthor{dewhirstModellingPerceivedSpatial2008} compares several methods to estimate \gls{asw} given \gls{ild}, \gls{itd}, and \gls{iacc} cues from binaural audio signals. The prediction results were promising, but mixed. \citeauthor{dewhirstModellingPerceivedSpatial2008} even notes that the original values of \gls{asw} given by test participants were somewhat varied, concluding that the test itself was a difficult task.

Since these early experiments the analysis of \gls{asw} has remained a topic of interest, with \citeauthorandnumber{kasbachAssessingModelingApparent2016}, \citeauthorandnumber{miethObjectiveEvaluationTechniques2017}, and \citeauthorandnumber{antoniukBlindEstimationEnsemble2023} all proposing ways to calculate it. It is not restricted to the binaural domain, with \citeauthorandnumber{sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008}, \citeauthorandnumber{wilsonPerceptionEvaluationAudio2013}, and \citeauthorandnumber{mourgelaExploringTrendsAudio2024} also proposing formulas for generic stereo signals. 

\gls{lev} on the other hand has prompted slightly more varied discussion over the years. An early study by \citeauthorandnumber{soulodreNewObjectiveMeasures2004} compared several objective measures for it. The methodology was to fit a correlational model that predicted the output of perceptual test from objective audio measures. The objective measures were based on either the \gls{iacc} or \gls{lef} of the audio signal. \citeauthor{soulodreNewObjectiveMeasures2004} notes that the late-arriving sound ($>80$ms after the sound's onset) are most relevant to \gls{lev}, regardless of which objective measure is used. \citeauthorandnumber{sunishDevelopmentValidationUnintrusive2010}, \citeauthorandnumber{powerInvestigationImpact3D2013}, \citeauthorandnumber{jacksonEstimatesPerceivedSpatial2010}, and \citeauthorandnumber{nowakPerceptionPredictionApparent2017} later expanded the same concept by fitting models on different objective audio measures. 

\citeauthor{dewhirstObjectiveAssessmentSpatial2005} propose a different measure in \cite{dewhirstObjectiveAssessmentSpatial2005} which involves calculating the area covered by the plurality of individual sound sources after estimating their directional localization. Although, by the authors' own admission, this is a crude measure and was not validated by perceptual tests.

Notably, the prediction of \gls{lev} appears to be used more often to assess spatial audio reproduction (sometimes rendered to binaural), rather than plain stereo. \citeauthorandnumber{powerInvestigationImpact3D2013} even includes both mono and stereo reproduction in their experiment, concluding that their subjective ratings for \gls{lev} were considerably lower than all spatial formats. \citeauthorandnumber{sunishDevelopmentValidationUnintrusive2010} also state that \gls{lev} might not be cleanly separable from \gls{asw}.

Within the context of this thesis, the absolute accuracy of any prediction is not necessarily of primary importance. Rather, it matters that the value calculated for each recording in a corpus provides suitable comparisons to the other recordings. \citeauthor{mourgelaExploringTrendsAudio2024}'s work on trends in music production \cite{mourgelaExploringTrendsAudio2024} is one example of such a study. The study groups raw stereo width calculations into 4 buckets, thereby showing that finding recordings with similar stereo width is of more interest than the absolute value.


\section{Spatial quality comparison}

The \glspl{sip} described in \cref{sec:spatial_cues,sec:stereo_image_description,sec:prediction_of_perceptual_scalars} are often used to assess the spatial degradation of an audio signal after some sort of distortion has been applied. A typical method is to calculate the same spatial descriptor on both a reference and degraded signal, then calculate the difference in those descriptors. There is sometimes an additional aggregation step to reduce a multidimensional descriptor down to a scalar.

The types of distortion vary, although a major industrial use is in the development of lossy codecs. The work of \citeauthorandnumber{youSpatialLocalizationEvaluation2012}, \citeauthorandnumber{wangObjectiveMeasurementSpatial2015}, \citeauthorandnumber{delgadoObjectiveMeasurementStereophonic2019}, and \citeauthorandnumber{delgadoObjectiveAssessmentSpatial2019} do exactly this. \Cref{fig:spatial_distortion} shows a typical workflow for the measurements of spatial distortion used in all these studies.

Spatial distortions other than lossy coding have also received attention in more recent years. For example, \citeauthor{barryBinaspectPythonLibrary2025} present a comparison of binaural rendering and downmix algorithms (as well as lossy codecs), showing that minute differences in binaural cues can be easily detected using their methods \cite{barryBinaspectPythonLibrary2025}. Taking the opposite approach, \citeauthor{watcharasupatQuantifyingSpatialAudio2024} demonstrates a new metric for assessing spatial quality and demonstrate it using several common, but artificial distortions \cite{watcharasupatQuantifyingSpatialAudio2024}.

In all the above studies, the detail and implementation of the spatial descriptors vary. Some studies (e.g. \cite{delgadoObjectiveAssessmentSpatial2019,barryBinaspectPythonLibrary2025}) opt to use high-level \glspl{sip} such as \q{directional loudness maps} or \gls{ild} histograms, while others (e.g. \cite{wangObjectiveMeasurementSpatial2015}) use low-level spatial cues such as \gls{ild} and \gls{itd}. In short, both approaches are valid and relevant to the \glspl{rq} presented in this thesis. 

Following from this establishment of objective measurements for spatial quality, it is increasingly common practice to use them as additional terms in \gls{dl} loss functions, thereby allowing the networks to learn spatial features. \citeauthorandnumber{erruzBinauralSourceSeparation2017} demonstrates a loss term based on the \gls{ild} of binaural audio, whereas both \citeauthorandnumber{limEnhancingSpatialAudio2024} and \citeauthorandnumber{giampiccoloModelingImpulseResponse2025} implement an \gls{icld} loss term for various combinations of ambisonic channels. In all these studies, the underlying industry motivation was the generation and rendering of new spatial audio formats, similar to the upmixing research mentioned in \cref{sec:history}.


\section{Analysis of large musical datasets}

Many of the studies cited in \cref{sec:stereo_image_description} use a large dataset of recorded music to demonstrate the effectiveness of their \glspl{sip}. For example, \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007,tzanetakisStereoPanningInformation2010,tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011} tackle genre recognition, \cite{ziemerAcousticFeaturesRecording2021} predicts artist/\glsxtrshort{dj}, and \cite{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011} determines the decade of the recordings. Similar studies such as \cite{zhouPredictingGeographicalOrigin2014,kedyteGeographicalOriginPrediction2017} show that the geographic origin of a recording can be determined from audio features, although they don't  include any spatial features. 

\citeauthor{ziemerProducerVsRapper2025}'s continuation of their work on goniometer features (see \cref{sec:stereo_image_description}) uses a dataset comprising songs that were either produced or rapped by specific artists \cite{ziemerProducerVsRapper2025}. They train a \gls{som} on the goniometer features of the dataset. The results firstly show that specific artists have specific sonic profiles. Furthermore, they show that when those artists collaborate, the sound profile of the resulting track is dominated by the producer.

In \citeauthor{panteliCharacterizationSingingStyles2017}'s work on the classification of singing style, the classes -- instead of being determined by an a-priori known label -- are learned by first extracting low-level audio cues, then finding common features within those cues. The features are then used as the input to unsupervised clustering algorithms to determine the relationships of the recordings to each other. \citeauthor{panteliComputationalStudyOutliers2017} continued the work in \cite{panteliComputationalStudyOutliers2017} where outliers in the global dataset where found using similar techniques and links between different musical cultures were drawn. On a cautionary note, \citeauthor{chauhanCrossCulturalBiasMelScale2026} recently showed that \gls{ml}-based tasks taking audio input from multiple cultures are biased towards Western sources if they use the Mel spectrogram as the audio frontend \cite{chauhanCrossCulturalBiasMelScale2026}. The authors recommend using alternative frontends, such as \gls{erb}.

\citeauthor{collinsNewCuratedCorpus2018} collated a dataset of historical electronic music \cite{collinsNewCuratedCorpus2018}. As part of that work, they extracted two spatial metrics: \q{channel loudness difference} and \q{stereo spatial ebb}. The example experiment given to demonstrate the dataset showed that \q{stereo spatial ebb} changes according to the year of the recording, thus indicating an artistic trend over time. \q{Channel  loudness difference}, on the other hand, remained static.

What all these studies have in common is an interest in the dataset itself regarding both the relationships between individual recordings, and the relationship of the entire corpus to the wider world. Low-level audio features are of lesser importance, other than to construct the high-level metrics that enable comparison between recordings.



\chapter{Research Methodology}
% How do music producers use stereo field?

% RQ1: How does the stereo image of a recording relate to real-
% world musical knowledge?

% RQ2: What methods can be used to describe the stereo image of
% a music recording?

In this chapter I break down the reasoning behind the \glspl{rq} given in \cref{sec:research_questions}, both from philosophical and practical standpoints. I argue that the methods I chose for the research in this thesis allow for a timely and achievable answer to the overarching \gls{rq}.

The overarching \gls{rq} -- \q{How to music producers use stereo field} -- could be approached in many ways. However, the work in this thesis is steeped in the context of large audio datasets. Indeed, the reason for performing the research at the current point in time is the ease of access to said datasets along with the availability of computer processing power to analyse them. From an ontological standpoint this most closely fits the Parmenidean viewpoint of \textit{being}. According to \citeauthor{grayDoingResearchReal2008}, Parmenidean ontology posits that reality is composed of clearly formed entities with identifiable properties \cite{grayDoingResearchReal2008}. If the producers' collective musical output is \q{reality}, then the recordings in the datasets certainly fit that description.

The two smaller \glspl{rq} represent different epistemological perspectives on the overarching one. \rqa{} is based in \textit{constructivism}, where the meaning of the stereo field is imposed on the recordings by the listener. Conversely, \rqb{} is firmly in the \textit{objectivism} camp, where the properties of a recording's stereo field are an objective truth that can be measured. Not only that, but generalizations about the use of stereo field across the entire population of recordings are also objective and measurable. While the two epistemologies are somewhat adverse to each other, they both fall under the ontology of \textit{being} \cite{chiaProductionManagementKnowledge2002}.

Under \textit{constructivism}, one valid research methodology is \q{heuristic inquiry} -- the act of totally immersing oneself in the phenomenon being researched and allowing intuition to guide the understanding of the \gls{rq}. I used this technique for a sizable part of the work described in this thesis. For example, {paper~\ref{paper:auxtrument}} is a retrospective account of building a new musical instrument where the primary sonic material was the signal from a remote stereo microphone. The act of building the instrument, listening to its output, and forming ideas about which sonic phenomena were interesting was a direct way to relate the stereo field with musical knowledge, addressing \rqa. While this provides a basis for other research, critics of heuristic inquiry will point out that it is deeply personal and therefore not particularly generalizable \cite{grayDoingResearchReal2008}.

To complement the \textit{constructive} approach and form more robust conclusions about an entire population of music producers, I concurrently embarked on a more objective research path. The epistemology of \textit{operationalism} is defined by the principle that in order to understand something, it must be measurable \cite{changOperationalism2021}. Thus, to understand stereo field, a set of operations to measure it is necessary. In practice, that means a software library capable of analysing music recordings and extracting information about their stereo field. The benefits of creating this software are threefold. Firstly, it enables comparative measurements of disparate music recordings, allowing one to validate and challenge the existing research described in \cref{sec:related_works}. Secondly, the methods used for any experimental research must be controllable and repeatable. A software library is a fantastic way to achieve this. Thirdly, the act of writing the library can be seen as a similar act of immersion to that previously described in the \textit{constructive} research. By breaking down the measurement of stereo field into simple instructions, one is forced to think about quantifiable relationships between audio phenomena. \citeauthor{wiltschkoLanguageThoughtCommunication2022} argues that language is a tool for both thought and communication in equal measure \cite{wiltschkoLanguageThoughtCommunication2022}. It follows that computer code (which is a written language like any other) acts as both a representation of the creator's thought process, and the communication of those thoughts to any readers. The software written for this thesis was therefore fundamental to the research process, as well as underpinning the results of papers \ref{paper:dafx} and \ref{paper:bth}.

One potential criticism of this combinatorial approach is that an answer to the overarching \gls{rq} could instead be gained through subjective means, e.g. interviews with music producers. This would quickly run into several problems, however. The first is that of scale. Subjective research is a time-consuming process and the resulting data is deep, but with few examples. The end research results might then not be generalizable to the entire population of music producers. Secondly, the music community holds an inherent understanding that music is a form of language \cite{agawuMusicDiscourseSemiotic2014}. Adding a layer of indirection (interviews conducted in spoken language) to the research process only serves to muddy the data. Indeed, \citeauthor{hospersConceptArtisticExpression1954} argues that an artist's expression is inherently intertwined with the creation the original art form, noting that the intentions are sometimes so complex and multifaceted as to defy explanation even to the artist themselves \cite{hospersConceptArtisticExpression1954}. In short, it makes most sense to analyse musical output directly.

The work I have performed for this thesis is a combination of immersion in musical practice, thorough reviews of existing literature, and detailed implementation of audio \gls{dsp} analysis techniques. This combination presents a well-rounded viewpoint on the original \glspl{rq}, as well as giving rise to new questions that could be answered in the continuation to doctoral studies (see \cref{sec:future_work}).

\chapter{Discussion and future work}
\label{sec:future_work}
The work documented in this licentiate thesis provides the theoretical and practical basis for analysis of stereo image in music recordings. The focus was on amassing the signal processing techniques that enable the analysis of stereo field, and testing their applicability to various types of recordings that were known a-priori. The next stage of research will involve applying the knowledge from this thesis on much larger musical corpora. The signal processing techniques will be honed and run on vast musical datasets, secure in the knowledge that they will pick out the appropriate information to the experiment at hand.

In particular, it is theorized that examining the \glspl{sip} of extremely large musical corpora will reveal patterns in production and recording techniques that were otherwise unobservable. It will also reveal the scale and variation of stereo techniques that musicians are able to employ in their work. Given the basic knowledge gained from the work in this thesis, it might then be possible to reason about how musicians and producers sculpt their stereo fields, relating it both to the musical content and the wider context in which it was created.

During the licentiate, several points arose that provoked further investigation. Firstly, the available public datasets of recorded music are generally encoded using lossy compression. While lossy compression algorithms undergo perceptual tests in their development, it is unclear how they affect spatial cues at the scale of said datasets (which encompass much musical variation). The spatial effects of lossy compression therefore warrant further investigation. Secondly, the public datasets are generally formed from web services where musicians have uploaded their own recordings for free. In contrast, professional recordings (which are much more numerous) are not included in any public datasets. Since professional recordings make up the vast majority of recorded music, it needs to assessed whether public datasets are representative of professional recordings.

In short, the doctoral research will tackle a larger problem of assessing human musical creativity though the recorded output. The techniques surfaced by the licentiate will provide the foundation, and will be applied to real-world data.
