\textbf{Proposed abstract} -- The \glsxtrfull{sip} of a music recording is an objective representation of the recording's stereo field. It is strongly tied to the artist and producers' musical intentions, while remaining distinct from precise musical content. Finding recordings with similar \glspl{sip} can be useful for e.g. music recommendation systems, or as a precursor to downstream audio processing. This paper presents a cluster analysis of the \glspl{sip} of a very large music corpus. The resulting clusters are examined to determine the specific musical and recording features surfaced by the clustering, and an assessment of the successfulness of the clustering is presented.

\section{Introduction}

Extracting spatial information from audio signals has a variety of uses, which generally fall into two broad categories. There is detailed spatial information that is specific to the exact audio clip analysed, and is generally used for a task that requires reconstruction of the original signal such as audio coding, compression, upmixing, and source separation. Secondly, there are methods used to describe the spatial properties of a signal, without the need for reconstruction. These are generally used for \gls{mir}, classification, or interpretation of audio. Industrial uses the latter and not as clear-cut as the former, but the literature gives uses such as music similarity/recommendation \cite{gomezMusicSimilaritySystems2008,ziemerNovelRecordingStudio2020}, or a precursor to upmixing, source separation, and audio enhancement \cite{harmaStereoAudioClassification2011,harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2011}.

In the aforementioned cases, the audio analysed has either comprised single excerpts or lists of 1000-2000 tracks with labels assigned. However, in recent years much bigger musical datasets have become available, e.g. \cite{bogdanovMTGJamendoDatasetAutomatic2019,defferrardFMADatasetMusic2017}, which contain 55000 and 100000 tracks respectively. These datasets do not contain any metadata regarding spatial information, nor any similar information such as recording methods/style. While they do offer genre and date tags, panning features on their own have previously shown low correlation with genre and decade \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningInformation2010,tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011}, from which we can infer that genre and date cannot be reliably used a proxy for panning features. If one wishes to use these datasets for the aforementioned use cases, there is hence a need to perform unsupervised learning on the extracted panning features, and for this to be successful we must understand the abilities and limits of the extraction method being used. 

The current spatial descriptors can be roughly classified thus:

\begin{itemize}
    \item High-dimensional descriptors - Spatial information extracted from a signal based on some sort of assumed audio properties (e.g. panning parameters, phase spread)
    \item Perceptual scalars - Descriptors that relate directly to a single value that has arisen from the results of perceptual test (e.g. width, envelopment)
    \item Objective scalars - Spatial information that has a measurable \enquote{correct answer} (e.g. direction-of-arrival)
\end{itemize}

\noindent The audio signals being analysed also fall into categories, and each of the above descriptors can be extracted from any category.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stereo - An audio signal with 2 channels
    \item Binaural - A subset of stereo that has either been recorded or synthesized under conditions that match/mimic human hearing
    \item Multi-channel audio - many individual channels that have been mixed and rendered at the creator side. Typical configurations are \enquote{5.1} (5 surround, 1 \gls{lfe}) and \enquote{7.1.4} (7 surround, 1 \gls{lfe}, 4 height).
    \item Microphone arrays - microphones arranged in specific patterns that together provide more spatial information than a single microphone
    * Binaural audio is special case of multichannel, where 2 channels represent sound at a person's ears. It therefore has specific statistical qualities that can inform any research.
    \item Ambisonics - a format capturing an overall physical sound field using \enquote{spherical harmonics}, i.e. sound pressure in various directions at a single point. The simplest form is called \enquote{1st order} and has 4 channels (omnidirectional, X, Y, Z) but research suggests that this is not sufficient to capture enough sound information for good rendering. More directions can be added, but the number of channels increases exponentially with the order.
    \item Object based audio - Instead of rendering channels, individual sound objects are stored separately along with metadata telling a renderer where to place them in a virtual space. This is currently the most common form of audio being researched and has the most practical use cases, especially in film/TV.
\end{itemize}

The most common and most well-researched is binaural audio since it is closest to human hearing. It also has the advantage that any other format can be rendered to binaural, given a set of assumptions as to the listening conditions. However, these assumptions don't always hold (e.g. when listening on headphones), so there are other methods that act directly on the audio signals. In those cases, separate methods for each flavour of audio signal are required.

Note: The only type of audio signal not included in this list is mono, even though some researchers argue that there is still spatial information in things such as reverberation.


\section{Problem statement}

The overarching research question can be described as:

\begin{itemize}
\item How can one predict the spatial properties of a music track under specified reproduction conditions?
\end{itemize}

\noindent This can be broken into several smaller problems:

\begin{enumerate}
\item What methods can be used to describe the stereo image of music recording?
\item What is the gamut of stereo images that exist in the entire population of recorded music?
\item What patterns can be found by examining the spatial information of a very large musical corpus?
\end{enumerate}

\section{State of the art}

\cite{bergSystematicEvaluationPerceived2003} specifies a long list of spatial attributes perceived by listeners, but three major ones often referred to by other authors are \enquote{ensemble source width} (sometimes called \enquote{apparent source width}), \enquote{extent of immersion} (sometimes called \enquote{listener envelopment}), and \enquote{extent of reverberation} \cite{soulodreNewObjectiveMeasures2004,sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008,sarroffComputationalModelPerceived2011}. \cite{ziemerUsingPsychoacousticModels2016} and \cite{ziemerSourceWidthMusic2017a} define a scalar measurement of \enquote{spaciousness} (which is very closely related to source width). Other lower-level scalar features exist, such as the \gls{simpl} feature which is used in \cite{chenMixedStereoAudio2014} to aid the classification of speech vs music. 

The above perceptual qualities are often inferred using objective measurements, which aim to describe the physical properties of an audio signal (usually in higher dimensionality) than the perceptual scalars.
\cite{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002,avendanoFrequencydomainSourceIdentification2003} are seminal works describing the \enquote{panning index} -- a description of amplitude panning for each time-frequency bin in a stereo spectrogram. \cite{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002} also presents the \enquote{ambience index} -- a measure of inter-channel correlation in \gls{tf} bins. While they target re-synthesis and upmix as use cases, the underlying techniques have become the basis for many stereo image descriptors since. 
\cite{barrySoundSourceSeparation2004} is another seminal work which describes a frequency-azimuth plane constructed from phase cancellation of different frequency components. Again, it targets re-panning and re-synthesis by assigning time-frequency bins to individual sources rather than stereo image description.
While not specifically the subject of this paper, for a comparison of similar techniques for directional segmentation and re-synthesis, the reader is referred to \cite{wellsComparisonAnalysisResynthesis2011}.
\cite{kimQuantifyingAlbumEffect2006} states that analysis of a recording's stereo field can be useful for classification, but does not give any techniques to measure it.
\cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007,tzanetakisStereoPanningInformation2010} reuse the panning index from \cite{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002} (renamed to \enquote{\gls{sps}}) and derive a set of \gls{spf} that are used for genre classification and \gls{mir}.
Similarly, \cite{sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008} details a method to produce stereo image descriptors centred around a histogram of spatial energy, but uses the frequency-azimuth plane from \cite{barrySoundSourceSeparation2004} as its basis.
Another histogram method, this time based on the \gls{sps} from \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007} is described in \cite{gomezMusicSimilaritySystems2008,guausAudioContentProcessing2009}, citing measurement of music similarity as the primary use case.
In a similar vein, and citing classification as a use case, \cite{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011} combines cochleagram differences with an analysis of phase differences between channels to form two feature sets - the \enquote{\gls{asf}} and \enquote{\gls{psf}}.

\cite{harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2010,harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2011,harmaStereoAudioClassification2011} collectively define a new paradigm of classifying stereo field whereby stereo mixtures are modelled as combinations of centre, panned, and uncorrelated sources; Although, they specifically state that most music signals will fall into a class that is very difficult to identify and is therefore omitted from the model.
\cite{prestiVisualizationManipulationStereophonic2014} uses the \gls{iid} and \gls{ipd} of an audio signal to derive bin-level measurements of \enquote{panning position} and \enquote{panning clarity}, which are subsequently used for sonogram visualization and manipulation.
Similar to the phase-based features defined in \cite{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011}, there are a group of algorithms based on the emulation of a studio goniometer - \cite{ziemerAcousticFeaturesRecording2021,ziemerGoniometersArePowerful2023} use both a goniometer box-counting technique and inter-channel correlation analysis to derive a time-varying measurement of spaciousness. Interestingly though, \cite{stirnatSpaciousnessMusicTonmeisters2019} found that subjective impressions of spaciousness in various musical styles were largely contradictory to the results of goniometer analysis.

\gls{dirac} is a technique that converts ambisonic audio to a representation of directionality and diffuseness \cite{pulkkiSpatialSoundReproduction2007}. While it can be used on stereo audio, the conversion from stereo to ambisonic assumes the use of speakers rather than headphones \cite{laitinenConvertingTwochannelStereo2014}.

More recently a \gls{ml}-based technique for disentangling spatial information has been demonstrated with a use case of upmixing stereo to multichannel \cite{yangUpmixingStyleTransfer2022}; The input to the system is a combination of multichannel audio and it's associated stereo downmix, which is then re-upmixed by a neural network. A latent vector formed by running multichannel audio through one half of the network is demonstrated to represent the spatial information contained in the original signal. 



\section{State of practice}

Even though all the audio formats mentioned contain spatial information, the industry uses the term \enquote{spatial audio} to mean multichannel, ambisonic, or object-based. The main industrial use for rendered audio is compression and transmission/distribution, and the formats that have emerged generally allow for all 3 audio types to be used as input, even if they will not necessarily remain in the same format when a file is produced.

The most common industry format is Dolby Atmos - originally designed for cinemas and other such very large speaker arrays, there is now focus in rendering it in headphones for consumer users. Headphone rendering sometimes entails the use of head tracking to make it appear as though audio is always coming from a specific direction, although this is generally limited to specific hardware platforms where tracking data is available. Even though Atmos is being heavily pushed by big industry players, adoption by consumers is slow. An international standard spatial format exists in the form of MPEG-H \cite{mpeg-h}, (and its branded version \enquote{Sony 360}), but adoption of them is even lower. DTS:X is another proprietary spatial audio format, although its usage is generally limited to films released on physical media \cite{publishedDTSWhatIt2021}. Use of the industry formats is sometimes problematic due to patent licensing, and as a response the Alliance for Open Media has also specified a \enquote{patent-free} standard for immersive audio named \gls{imaf} \cite{ImmersiveAudioModel}.
