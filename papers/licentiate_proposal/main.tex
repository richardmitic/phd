\section{Introduction}

It is a widely accepted truth in the world of music production and recording that spatial information in audio is crucial to  listeners' sense of immersion and connection with the music. To capture that spatiality, stereophonic recording and reproduction has been the standard format since it gained popularity in the 1950s \cite{lanierHIFITWOCHANNELCOMMOTION1957}. The spatial analysis of subsequent decades worth of stereo recordings presents a unique opportunity to study human musicality, as each recording reflects and encodes the creative choices made by the musicians and recordists involved.

Within the music community, it is common to speak about spatiality in a qualitative sense, and there have been several attempts to translate these discussions in a quantitive domain \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007,dewhirstModellingPerceivedSpatial2008,sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008,tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011}. All of these studies were performed in an era when musical datasets were hand-curated/-labelled, fairly small (1,000 - 2,000 tracks), and -- most notably -- private. However, in recent years much larger public musical corpora have become available, e.g. MTG-Jamendo \cite{bogdanovMTGJamendoDatasetAutomatic2019} and \gls{fma} \cite{defferrardFMADatasetMusic2017}, which contain approximately 55,000 and 100,000 tracks respectively. In addition, computing techniques in \gls{ml} and \gls{dl} have progressed to the point where it is now possible to learn extremely high-level features on these vast datasets, thereby making sense of the human creativity represented in the recordings. 

A typical use case for spatial descriptors that has emerged both in academia and industry is classification, and in particular, auto-tagging of musical genre/performer \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningInformation2010,ziemerGoniometersArePowerful2023}. Such features can be used in music similarity and recommendation tasks \cite{gomezMusicSimilaritySystems2008,ziemerNovelRecordingStudio2020}. It has also been shown that stereo field classification of subsections within a larger work can act as a precursor to other tasks such as audio enhancement, upmixing, and source separation \cite{harmaStereoAudioClassification2011,harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2011}. The latter is very relevant to the audio distribution industry, which is currently experimenting with newer audio formats such as ambisonic and object-based audio. While these new formats offer exciting opportunities for both distributors and listeners, there remains a huge back-catalogue of stereophonic recordings that could benefit from a high-level understanding of their spatial content. 

Historically, binaural audio (which is a subclass of stereo) has been of particular interest because its relevance to human hearing. Thus, it is extremely widely studied and the underlying fundamental techniques are well-established. There remains however, a gap in current research regarding direct analysis of raw music recordings, and in particular, describing the stereo image of said recordings at a high level. This thesis aims to fill that gap. It will firstly demonstrate use cases where the spatial analysis of stereo audio has the opportunity to solve real-world problems in audio transmission, dissemination, and reproduction. Accordingly, it will gather existing methods to describe stereo image and assess their abilities to detect the spatial aspects of a variety of musical audio sources.

How do music producers use stereo field?
\end{quote}

\item What methods can be used to describe the stereo image of music recording?
\end{enumerate}

\section{State of the art}

The psychoacoustic theory behind spatial hearing has been an active area of research since the mid 20th century, with early examples including the Jeffress model in which time differences between sound events at each ear contribute to a person's ability to locate sounds in space \cite{jeffressPlaceTheorySound1948}. Lindemann's work a few decades later \cite{lindemannEvaluationInterauralSignal1982} expanded on this idea by translating the earlier theory to a \gls{dsp} context, and establishing the \gls{ild}, \gls{itd}, and \gls{iacc} as important measures to describe the spatial aspects of binaural signals in a quantifiable way.

These low-level spatial cues have formed the basis of many other algorithms since. One such use is the prediction of perceptual ratings given by subjects in response to concert hall acoustics or the audio quality of music reproduction systems. \Citeauthor{bergSystematicEvaluationPerceived2003} \cite{bergSystematicEvaluationPerceived2003} give a long list of typical perceptual measures, although later literature has focused on the measures of \gls{asw} and \gls{lev}. However, as stated by \citeauthor{sarroffComputationalModelPerceived2011} \cite{sarroffComputationalModelPerceived2011}, the task of describing the spatial aspects of recorded music is inherently different to that acoustical signals since said recordings may exhibit spatial aspects that cannot occur in physical acoustic space.

Early examples of the use of spatial cues for analysis of recorded music are the \enquote{\gls{pi}} and \enquote{ambience index} specified by \citeauthor{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002} \cite{avendanoFrequencyDomainTechniques2002}, which are loosely analogous to \gls{ild} and \gls{iacc} in the acoustics domain. To estimate the \gls{pi}, a signal model is assumed whereby a musical source is panned within the mix using the \enquote{energy preserving pan law}, in which amplitude differences are applied to each channel such that the sum of the squared amplitudes equals 1. While this panning technique does not affect the phase of either channel, existing phase differences between channels are measured using the \enquote{ambience index}, which is defined such that it is low when the stereo channels are correlated (indicating a direct sound source), and high when channels are uncorrelated (indicating an ambient or reverberant sound source). The same signal model has been the basis for much musical research since, e.g. the \gls{adress} algorithm \cite{barrySoundSourceSeparation2004} (and its subsequent downstream projects \cite{cooneyEnhancedImplementationADRess2006,sofianosEffectiveSingingVoice2010,defreinReformulatingBinaryMasking2020,defreinRemedyingSoundSource2020}) which attempts to separate sound sources by locating them in the horizontal plane.

Using the same signal model, the spatial description of recorded music specifically has been attempted several times. Firstly by Tzanetakis et al. in \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007,tzanetakisStereoPanningInformation2010}, who use the \gls{pi} to define a set of \gls{spf} used for genre classification and \gls{mir}. \gls{spf} firstly splits the mix into perpetually relevant frequency bands, secondly describes the \enquote{amount of panning} present in each band, and optionally estimates the number of sources present in the mix. For a more detailed inspection of precisely where in the horizontal plane musical energy is located, one can instead utilize a \gls{ph} using the techniques described by \citeauthorandnumber{sarroffMeasurementsSpaciousnessStereophonic2008} or \citeauthorandnumber{gomezMusicSimilaritySystems2008}.

While the aforementioned studies focus on descriptions of amplitude panning in stereo mixes, recording engineers can make use of other techniques such as to add spatial feeling to their mix. These techniques (described by \citeauthor{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011} as \enquote{Phase Stereo}) can be quantified using their definition of the \gls{ssps} \cite{tardieuProductionEffectAudio2011}. In the same manuscript another method of quantifying amplitude panning is defined, which is based on the \gls{ild} of a cochleagram, and aims to contain more precision in the frequency axis than the azimuthal axis.

An alternative signal model is proposed by \citeauthor{harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2010} \cite{harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2010,harmaClassificationTimefrequencyRegions2011,harmaStereoAudioClassification2011}, whereby a stereo mix is composed of combinations of centre, panned, and uncorrelated sources. Their subsequent classification of stereo field differentiates between different combinations of those source types.

All the above use classical \gls{dsp} to create their spatial descriptors, but more recently there have been efforts to use \gls{dl} to disentangle spatial information from audio content. One such example was demonstrated by \citeauthorandnumber{yangUpmixingStyleTransfer2022}. While the original aim was to upmix stereo music to multichannel, a necessary component of the resulting neural network was a latent space that represents the spatial information of the multichannel signal. \gls{dl} has also been used outside the music domain to e.g. learn spatial relationships between individual microphones in an array \cite{yangSelfSupervisedLearningSpatial2024}. While such tasks do not directly address the research questions for this thesis, they demonstrate that learning spatial features on large amounts of unlabelled audio data is possible given appropriate methods and pretext task.

Neural generation of spatial audio continues to be a hot topic up to the present day, with many attempts to generate high-quality binaural, ambisonic or multichannel audio from mono sources \cite{limEnhancingSpatialAudio2024,liCyclicLearningBinaural2024,liTASPersonalizedTextguided2024,chenCCStereoAudioVisualContextual2025,melechovskySonicMasterControllableAllinOne2025,santosLargeScaleSelfSupervisedLearning2025,wangSpatialV2AVisualGuidedHighfidelity2026}. In doing so, the networks must infer spatial information from a source other than audio (typically an accompanying video or text prompt), and in the process will learn the spatial qualities of their audio training data.


\section{State of practice}

In industry, spatial analysis of music signals is most commonly used for audio coding, compression, and transmission/distribution. International standards for audio coding include MPEG-Surround \cite{iso/iec23003-1InformationTechnologyMPEG2007}, which encodes multichannel audio based on the spatial cues \gls{ild}, \gls{ipd}, and \gls{iacc}. However, there has recently been a shift away from channel-based audio (such as MPEG-Surround) to scene- and object-based paradigms. In fact, the subsequent \gls{mpeg} standard MPEG-H \cite{mpeg-h} includes options to encode all three audio paradigms. The most common industry format is currently Dolby Atmos\texttrademark. Originally designed for cinemas and other such very large speaker arrays, there is now focus on rendering it in headphones for consumer users. Headphone rendering sometimes entails the use of head tracking to make it appear as though audio is always coming from a specific direction, although this is generally limited to specific hardware platforms where tracking data is available. Most recently, \gls{3gpp} have begun standardization of \gls{ivas} \cite{IVASCodecNG}, a telephony standard which again, includes all the aforementioned audio paradigms.

Even though the newer audio formats are being heavily pushed by big industry players, adoption by consumers and music professionals is slow.  Adoption of MPEG-H and its branded version \enquote{Sony 360} is even lower. \enquote{DTS:X} is another proprietary spatial audio format, although its usage is generally limited to films released on physical media \cite{publishedDTSWhatIt2021}. Use of the industry formats is sometimes problematic due to patent licensing, and as a response the Alliance for Open Media has also specified a \enquote{royalty-free} standard for immersive audio named \gls{iamf} \cite{ImmersiveAudioModel}. Within the domain of spatial audio generation, \gls{vr} and \gls{ar} are the most common use cases given, although they are also yet to find mainstream adoption.

A common use case given in academia for calculating stereo features of individual tracks is music similarity detection and recommendation. However, the industry has generally not adopted these techniques, opting instead to use textural and contextual data for each track.


\section{Research Methods}
The papers mentioned in \cref{sec:papers} used (or will use) the following research methods:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\nameref{paper:A}}: Several \gls{dsp} techniques for determining audio spatial features were performed on a small dataset that had predictable musical characteristics. The different techniques were compared using a new objective metric.
    \item \textbf{\nameref{paper:B}}: A perceptual test was used to determine listeners' reactions to carefully constructed audio stimuli. Standard statistical analysis was performed on the raw test results, and one particular \gls{dsp} technique (\gls{ph} \cite{gomezMusicSimilaritySystems2008} which was assessed in \nameref{paper:A}) was used to explain the perceptual results.
    \item \textbf{\nameref{paper:C}}: Following on the small selection of \gls{dsp} techniques assessed in \nameref{paper:A}, a \gls{slr} following the \gls{prisma} standard was performed to gather all currently available methods for determining spatial features of stereophonic musical audio.
    \item \textbf{\nameref{paper:D}}: Based on the results of the previous three papers, several types of \gls{sip} will be extracted from a very large dataset of recordings, and basic unsupervised machine-learning will be applied to find patterns within the \gls{sip} population.
\end{itemize}


\section{Preliminary results and conclusions}

\nameref{paper:A} revealed that although there are several \gls{dsp} techniques specifically designed for the extraction of recording and panning features, they are all limited in their own ways. One particular technique (\gls{spf} from \cite{tzanetakisStereoPanningFeatures2007}) was particularly promising as a candidate for use in \nameref{paper:D}, and the others could be used with some modifications. Overall it highlighted the importance of choosing both a panning feature \gls{dsp} technique and a distance metric that are appropriate to the overarching task.

\nameref{paper:B} concluded that when listeners are presented with voice overlayed on top of music, the best panning position for the voice was not always in the default centre position. The reasons for this were unclear due to a shortage of data, but the paper did demonstrate a real-world use of \gls{sip} extraction (using a algorithm trialed in \nameref{paper:A}). Most relevant to the research questions presented in \cref{sec:problem_statement}, it revealed that an objective measurement of panning can show detail that is not easily discernable by listening, such as very slight panning away from the centre.

\nameref{paper:C} revealed several more techniques that can be used to extract panning features. Although, given the high number of studies that were assessed for the review, there were relatively few very-relevant ones. The \gls{slr} also revealed that while \gls{ml} has been used on spatial features in the past, both \textit{unsupervised} \gls{ml} and very large music corpora remain fairly unexplored.

Overall the results so far have highlighted the known technique for extracting panning features from music, demonstrated the musical features that the major ones are able to reveal, and highlighted gaps in current research that \nameref{paper:D} aims to fill.

\section{Remaining work for licentiate}
Initially there is a need to find a new publication venue for \nameref{paper:B}, and this work is already underway. \nameref{paper:D} therefore comprises the bulk of the remaining work. Firstly it involves setting up a system that can gather \glspl{sip} en-mass from real recorded music. Secondly there will be an exploratory phase where many of the standard unsupervised learning techniques will be attempted to discover which are suitable for \gls{sip} data. The results of the \gls{ml} will then need to be assessed in some manner. One option is to correlate them with some known music metadata to reveal which other features are linked to panning. Another option is to perform listening test in order to assess is the \gls{ml} worked as intended.
